                                   1. INTRODUCTION

        During the summer of 2024, Pedro Chaviano, a master's candidate in electrical engineering
from Florida International University (FIU), joined the Chief Technology Office (CTO) at
Washington River Protection Solutions (WRPS) to contribute to a key research and development
project for the Tank and Pipeline Integrity (TAPI) organization, as outlined in the Technology
Element Description Summary (TEDS) MTW-89, which highlights the need for remote cleaning
solutions. This project focused on the design and development of a remotely operated robotic
vacuum crawler intended for the inspection and cleaning of the annulus space in double-shell tanks
(DSTs), Figure 1, at the DOE's Hanford site.




      Figure 1. (28) In-Service Underground Double-Shell Tanks constructed in 1968-1986


1.1 Project Overview
        Inspection of the double shell tanks include ultrasonic testing of the primary tank walls as
well as the secondary tank bottoms to test for wall thinning. The primary tank wall must be
corrosion free for the test to be conducted. Some of these tanks require extensive work to remove
corrosion on the outer surface of the primary tank before ultrasonic testing could be performed.
This results in significant amounts of debris on the annulus floor. This debris interferes with efforts
to perform ultrasonic testing on the secondary tank bottom. The annulus space, approximately 30
inches wide with access points ranging from 12 to 24 inches in diameter, presents significant
engineering challenges for robotic systems. Figure 1 depicts one of the 28 in-service DSTs
constructed between 1968 and 1986. The tanks accumulate debris over time, with some tanks
experiencing debris buildup up to 1 foot deep (Figure 2).

The vacuum crawler system (VCS), as outlined in section vii, includes various components
essential for its operation. The crawler is equipped with two EXAIR Line Vac pneumatic vacuum
modules mounted in series—one on the crawler itself and the other positioned higher up on the
umbilical harness. This module, integrated into the system, maximizes pull force and airflow at
the cleaning nozzle. The system features a durable design with components such as a heavy-duty
in-line vacuum module, advanced control capabilities including real-time video feedback with low
latency.

The crawler's robust architecture allows it to traverse obstacles and welds up to ½ inch in height,
through utilizing differential drive methods. The system’s protype chassis is crafted from
acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS) 3D printed plastic and will be upgraded to stainless steel,
anodized aluminum, or non-porous plastics, ensuring material compatibility with the nuclear
environment and meeting stringent radiation tolerance requirements.

Key features of the design include a pneumatic vacuum system, operated by an appropriately sized
air compressor rated at 185 scfm. The crawlers future state will include mechanical assemblies
designed to agitate surfaces and reduce particle sizes to ½ inch or less, enhancing the vacuum
efficiency and reducing airborne debris.

Pedro’s internship focused on addressing critical engineering challenges such as suction power,
filtration, maneuverability, and deployment strategies. Testing at the Cold Test Facility (CTF), as
shown in (Figure 4), demonstrated the crawler's ability to navigate complex geometries and
remove debris effectively. Feedback from stakeholders has led to plans for further refinement of
the system, including improvements to communication and power circuits and the construction of
a more robust metal chassis.

2.1 Deployment strategies

        The initial stage of the internship focused on developing a deployment method for the
crawler through the Mockup Riser at the Cold Test Facility (Figure 5). Key considerations included
maintaining a controlled descent speed and ensuring the crawler remains parallel to the floor to
prevent the front nozzle from crashing into the floor. This was achieved by integrating an
additional suspension cable, secured within the umbilical cord sleeve, to prevent entanglement
with the main safety suspension cable. This design ensures that the crawler can be manually
retrieved in the event of a mechanical failure.

A structure was designed to mount on the flange of the mockup riser, providing easy access to
hoist controls in case of entanglement during deployment (Figures 7-9).

end. The main connection includes the vacuum hose, electrical cable, leveling cable, and safety

2.2 Design
       The VCS is composed of several critical components, engineered to navigate and clean the
annulus space within DSTs at the Hanford site. The primary elements of the design include:

Pneumatic Vacuum Module:
The crawler is equipped with two Exair Line Vac pneumatic vacuum modules, positioned in series
to optimize suction power. One module is mounted directly on the crawler, while the other is
integrated further along the umbilical harness. This configuration ensures maximum airflow and
pull force at the cleaning nozzle, enhancing the crawler's debris collection capabilities.
An appropriately sized portable air compressor, rated at 185 scfm, provides the necessary flow
rate to operate the vacuum module.

Crawler Chassis and Mobility System:




                                  Figure 13. Chassis Prototype

The prototype chassis (Figure 13) is constructed from acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS) 3D-
printed plastic. However, future iterations will incorporate materials such as stainless steel,
anodized aluminum, or non-porous plastics to ensure compatibility with the nuclear environment
and meet radiation tolerance requirements.

The crawler employs a differential drive system, utilizing four independently controlled wheels to
navigate the 30-inch-wide annulus space. The system is designed to traverse obstacles such as
welds and conduits up to 1/2 inch in height, with rubber tread nubs on the wheels providing
enhanced grip and stability.

Control and Feedback Systems:

The system's control features will consist of bidirectional brush selectors, variable light controls,
and an intuitive joystick interface. Real-time video feedback with low latency is provided through
onboard cameras, allowing operators to control the crawler from a distance of up to 300 feet
(Figures 14-15).

The system operates without the need for software or firmware, relying instead on analog control
circuits to manage motor functions, video feedback, and other sensor data. This analog control
circuit approach minimizes potential points of failure and simplifies the system's operation in the
field.

2.3 Testing Procedures

       The VCS underwent rigorous testing at CTF to evaluate its performance under conditions
simulating the DST environment. Key testing procedures included:

Vacuum Performance Testing:
The suction power and airflow of the Exair Line Vac modules were tested using various aggregates
of debris and rocks. Tests were conducted on both 1” and 1-½” vacuum lines to validate the
system's ability to handle different debris sizes and types. The system’s ability to maintain suction
and prevent clogging was assessed by running the vacuum module continuously while navigating
the mock annulus.

Maneuverability Testing:
The crawler’s ability to traverse obstacles and navigate around welds up to ½ inch in height was
tested. The system’s design and ground clearance were evaluated to ensure reliable performance
on warped liner bottoms and slopes within the annulus (Figure 17). Differential drive control
systems were fine-tuned to optimize the crawler’s responsiveness and stability during operation.




                                  Figure 17. Control Room Camera Feed

Control System Validation:
The analog control circuits were tested and validated to ensure reliable operation without
dependencies on software or firmware. The joystick interface was programmed to control all
motors, and the system’s response time to operator inputs was measured to be within 1 second,
using a manual timer and adjustments to the joystick calibration.

                                  3. RESULTS AND ANALYSIS

            The VCS underwent a series of tests at CTF to evaluate its performance in simulating the
     conditions within the annulus space of DSTs. The key areas tested included suction power,
     maneuverability, debris removal efficiency, and control system responsiveness.

     Vacuum Module Configuration:
     The system used dual EXAIR Line Vac pneumatic vacuum modules arranged in series. These
     modules, connected to standard hoses or tubes, act as in-line conveyors, efficiently moving large
     volumes of material over long distances. Compressed air is injected through nozzles, creating a
     vacuum that generates high output flows. The design features a large, smooth bore to maximize
     material throughput, and its maintenance-free operation is ensured by the absence of moving parts.




                                       Figure 18. Collecting air flow data

     The conveying rate is influenced by various factors, including the size and mass of the material,
     as well as the length, lift, and bends in the hose or tube. Hands-on testing is crucial for optimizing
     configurations, such as selecting appropriate fittings, to ensure efficient flow rates (Figure 18).
     The data collected during these trials are shown in Table 1.

3.1 Analysis

       The test results indicate that the VCS demonstrates strong performance across several key
metrics, particularly in terms of vacuum efficiency and maneuverability.

Vacuum Efficiency:
The EXAIR Line Vac pneumatic vacuum modules proved highly effective in removing debris
from the annulus space. As shown in Table 1, debris removal percentages ranged from 22.0% to
95.0%, with the highest efficiency achieved using a combination of 1-inch and 1 ½-inch vacuum
lines at 15 feet. The increased diameter likely enhances airflow and reduces the risk of clogging,
offering valuable insights for future design optimizations.

Maneuverability:
The crawler successfully navigated the required obstacle height of 1/2 inch, validating the
robustness of the differential drive system. Maintaining consistent speed while overcoming
obstacles is crucial for reliable operation in the challenging annulus environment, as detailed in
Table 2. Future tests will be necessary to account for the additional weight from the metal chassis
redesign. Additionally, the crawler executed a full 360-degree turn within the 30-inch gap and
incline of the shell.

                                    4. CONCLUSION

        The development and testing of the vacuum crawler system successfully demonstrated its
capability to address the critical challenges associated with the inspection and cleaning of the
annulus space in Double-Shell Tanks (DSTs) at the DOE's Hanford site (Figure 23). The crawler
effectively utilized the dual Exair Line Vac pneumatic vacuum modules to achieve high debris
removal efficiency, while the robust design ensured reliable maneuverability over obstacles up to
½ inch in height. The control system provided precise operation, with low-latency video feedback
and accurate joystick control contributing to the overall effectiveness of the system. The crawler
effectively performed its primary function of debris removal, achieving removal rates of up to 95%
under various testing conditions, thus validating its design and operational capabilities.




                                    Figure 20. Demonstration

The success of the initial testing trials indicates that the vacuum crawler system (VCS) is well-
positioned to advance into further stages of development, with the potential to significantly
enhance the safety and efficiency of hazardous waste management in nuclear facilities. Insights
gained from these trials will guide the next steps in refining the system's design. Planned upgrades
include replacing the prototype chassis with a more robust design using stainless steel or anodized
aluminum to withstand the harsh conditions within double-shell tanks (DSTs). Further
development will improve vacuum efficiency, focusing on enhancing debris collection with
upgrades to the vacuum module and brush assemblies. The multistage mechanical system will be
refined to ensure reliable retrieval and containment of foreign objects, along with enhancements
to the brush assembly and debris container. Additionally, the control system will be upgraded with
added sensor data feedback, such as temperature, pressure, and flow, providing operators with
more comprehensive monitoring capabilities. These planned enhancements aim to optimize the
vacuum system and integrate additional mechanical assemblies for surface agitation, contributing
to a more effective and reliable hazardous waste management solution.

This project demonstrated the viability of using remotely operated robotic systems for complex
environmental management tasks, contributing to the broader mission of the U.S. Department of
Energy's environmental remediation efforts. With continued development, the VCS has the
capability to become a valuable tool in supporting the maintenance of nuclear storage facilities.
