                                 1 INTRODUCTION
During the summer of 2025, Ronald Verde, a mechanical engineering student from Florida
International University (FIU), joined the Chief Technology Officer (CTO) at Hanford Tank Waste
Operations & Closure (H2C) to contribute to a key research and development project for the Tank
and Pipeline Integrity (TAPI) organization. This project, which focuses on remote cleaning
solutions for the annulus space of double-shell tanks (DSTs), directly supports the U.S.
Department of Energy's environmental remediation goals. This work is executed in alignment with
the strategic vision of the Technology and Innovation Roadmap, RPP-PLAN-43988 (now in
Rev.09 [1]), which identifies the need for the development of robotic platforms and remote
technologies for radiological surveying, inspections, and manipulations to significantly reduce
human exposure to hazardous environments. The specific need for this remote cleaning solution is
detailed in the Technology Element Description Summary (TEDS) MTW-89, which highlights the
requirement for remote cleaning of concrete surfaces painted with a special protective coating
(SPC). The core of this project focused on the design and development of the remotely operated
robotic vacuum crawler intended for the inspection and cleaning of the annulus space in double-
shell tanks (DSTs).




         Figure 1. (28) The Underground Double-Shell Tanks constructed in 1968-1986

Hanford's Double-Shell Tanks (DSTs) are critical infrastructure for storing radioactive waste, and
their annular spaces must be regularly inspected and maintained to ensure structural integrity, as
shown in Figure 1. A primary challenge in this process is the accumulation of various forms of
debris which hinder crucial inspection procedures such as ultrasonic testing. The Annulus Vacuum
Crawler project was developed to address this issue by providing a robotic solution to navigate
these confined and hazardous spaces to remove debris, thereby enabling proper inspections. The
urgency of this project has been heightened by the introduction of new debris, specifically
powdered residue from recent cold spray repairs, which adds to the original debris on the annulus
floor.

1.1    Project Outline
Over time, Hanford’s Double Shell Tanks accumulated debris, with some tanks experiencing
buildup as deep as 1 foot, as shown in Figure 2. The annulus space approximately 30 inches wide
with access points ranging from 12 to 24 inches in diameter, presents significant engineering
challenges for robotic systems designed to operate within such confined environments.




               Figure 2. Debris Inside of the Annulus Double Shell Tank AY-101


This report examines the methods, testing processes, and project outcomes associated with annulus
waste management. It highlights the hazardous nature of the annulus space and its critical role in
maintaining nuclear site integrity. In alignment with the U.S. Energy Sector's Environmental
Management Program, continuous development efforts aim to improve the reliability, efficiency
and safety of the systems. The scope of the 2025 summer internship will focus on advancing the
Annulus Vacuum Robot from a laboratory-validated system to an operational-ready prototype.

                         2 RESEARCH DESCRIPTION

2.1 Deployment strategies

The initial stage of the internship focused on developing a deployment strategy for the crawler
through both the 24-inch and 12-inch Mockup Risers at the Cold Test Facility, as photographed in
Figure 3 . This deployment strategy, depicted in Figure 4, involves using an electric hoist to drop
the crawler face down. The design ensures that the crawler's wheels make initial contact with the
annulus floor, allowing it to drive forward and orient itself correctly upon landing. Key
considerations included managing the descent to ensure the wheels touch down first, thereby
preventing the front nozzle from impacting the floor. This method also allows for manual retrieval
using a safety suspension cable in the event of a mechanical failure.

The operators' exceptional skill in handling the hoist system was a major factor in the success of
our mock demonstrations. Their ability to make small, precise adjustments during the descent
made the process significantly smoother and more controlled. Furthermore, their established
control room, seen in Figure 5 , located 500 feet away from the tank, was equipped with an
external camera that provided them with enhanced visibility. This remote vantage point was
critical, allowing the team to carefully monitor the crawler's deployment and ensure a seamless
entry into the annulus.

A series of essential modifications were made directly to the crawler, eliminating the need for the
external harness that was previously required. A newly manufactured cage and an onboard bag for
debris collection were added to the crawler itself. This streamlined the entire system, making the
separate, suspended debris collector obsolete. As a result of these improvements, the main
connection to the crawler is now limited to just the vacuum hose, electrical cable, level cable, and
safety cable. This simplified setup improves deployment and retrieval efficiency, while also
minimizing the risk of entanglement within the confined space of the tank annulus.

2.2 Design
The project's primary objective is to develop a tool capable of removing debris from the Double-
Shell Tank annulus, thereby facilitating accurate and thorough inspection of the tank walls for
thickness and integrity. This work directly supports the Hanford Tank Waste Operations &
Closure (H2C) mission.

2.1.1   On-Board Debris Collection Cage

To achieve this, several key design components were developed and refined. The on-board debris
collection cage was designed for both strength and functionality. Its frame structure was built using
SolidWorks' 3D sketch capabilities, with swept features forming a curved top section to fit the 12-
inch riser. This design allows the operator to quickly and easily dispose of collected debris.

2.1.2   Advanced Hub Design

The hub assembly also underwent significant customization. The purchased hubs were too large,
prompting the team to design a compact, integrated solution in SolidWorks. This custom design
ensures a perfect fit with our specific wheel and motor shaft, providing direct and efficient power
transfer to the wheels.

2.1.3   Enhanced Chassis for Vacuum System

The chassis was redesigned to accommodate the new vacuum system and better navigate the tank
environment. Integrated channels were added to route airflow and wiring internally, minimizing
external hoses and snag hazards. The chassis also allows the crawler system to traverse conduits,
and modular service panels created using advanced surfacing techniques to provide easy
maintenance access to all internal components.

2.1.4   Redesigned Vacuum Head

The vacuum head was completely redesigned to improve performance. SolidWorks' cavity feature
and flow simulation analysis were used to create a multi-zone suction system with parametric
control for optimized debris pickup. The design included an adjustable height mechanism with
linear actuators and guide rails, allowing adaptation to uneven surfaces. Internal baffles, created
with swept cuts and lofted features, prevent oversized objects from clogging the system. The new
head is specifically sized to efficiently vacuum debris up to 1/4-inch in diameter and includes an
onboard collection bag to reduce travel distance for debris, increasing efficiency.

2.1.5   Crawler System Upgrades

Key upgrades during the internship included the fabrication of a new all-aluminum chassis,
replacing the previous 3D-printed plastic frame. This enhancement significantly increased the
crawler's durability and radiation tolerance. The system also features newly designed treaded
aluminum wheels for improved traction and the ability to cross obstacles up to ½ inch in height.
Its compact dimensions allow it to pass through both 12-inch and 24-inch riser access points, and
the design enables 180-degree maneuverability within the 30-inch-wide annular space, a critical
feature for effective navigation. To further enhance structural integrity and support the new
onboard debris collection system, a CNC-machined cage was manufactured to secure the
equipment bag. The chassis prototype is illustrated in Figure 6.




                                   Figure 6. Chassis prototype


2.3 Vacuum System Design & Development
The robot is built around a two-line vacuum configuration using two Exair Line Vac pneumatic
vacuum modules positioned in series to optimize suction power. One module is mounted directly
on the crawler, while the other is integrated on the top of the crawler itself. This design ensures
maximum airflow and pulls force at the cleaning nozzle. During development, the coupling size
was changed from 3/8" to 1/2" to increase the volume of suction. For the demonstration, a 1-inch
hose line was used, which was then connected to a 1/2-inch hose line leading to the crawler. An
appropriately sized portable air compressor as seen in Figure 7 rated at 185 standard cubic feet per
minute (SCFM), provides the necessary flow rate.

FIU-ARC-2025                                                           Double Shell Annulus Crawler Vacuum




                             Figure 7. Air Compressor and Air Lines.

The vacuum crawler system (VCS) is built around powerful vacuum architecture. The previous
design configuration, which had one module on the umbilical harness, has been superseded. In the
modified model, two EXAIR Line Vac pneumatic vacuum modules are now installed in series and
onboard the crawler itself. This was made possible by new alterations to the chassis and the design
of a CNC-machined cage that allows the debris collection bag to be mounted directly on the
crawler. This system-integrated module maximizes both vacuum force and airflow at the cleaning
nozzle. The pneumatic vacuum system is powered by an appropriately sized portable air
compressor, rated at 185 SCFM, which provides the necessary flow rate.

2.4 Electronics & Controls

Completely modernized the system's electronics and controls by implementing a new, custom-
designed Printed Circuit Board (PCB). This PCB created a highly organized and reliable
connection between the main components, including the Raspberry Pi, Teensy, RoboClaw motor
controllers, and the motors themselves. This consolidated architecture eliminates the traditional
mess of wires and significantly enhances signal integrity and overall system stability.

The new control interface features a custom control box, seen in Figure 8, with an integrated touch
screen that runs on a Linux with Ubuntu operating system. This setup provides a professional and
intuitive user experience. Replaced the traditional joystick with a Logitech controller for simplified
and precise operation. This allows for seamless control of the robot, its cameras, and the on-board
lights. The touch screen also provides a platform for displaying live, real-time video feedback,
giving the operator crucial situational awareness.

To manage the system's functions, developed specialized ROS2 (Robot Operating System 2)
nodes. These nodes are the core of our control logic, handling everything from the motor
functions for the wheels to the servo motor that controls the camera's movement. Used "joy
nodes" and "telejoy nodes" to translate inputs from the Logitech controller into precise
commands for the robot. A separate ROS2 node was created for our dual-camera setup, and a
dedicated launch script was developed to configure and activate all these nodes simultaneously.
This robust integration allows for real-time navigation and remote control, all supported by a
carefully designed power management system that ensures extended operational time.

Facility (CTF) to evaluate its performance under conditions simulating the annulus space of the
DSTs. These evaluations focused on the system’s core subsystems: vacuum performance,
maneuverability, and control system responsiveness. Deployment strategies were tested in a
mock DST environment, seen in Figure 9, and the crawler’s onboard microcontrollers were
assessed for reliability and integration, seen in Figure 10.

two-line Exair Line Vac modules, which were mounted in series on a newly designed aluminum

3.1 Maneuverability Testing
The crawler's ability to traverse obstacles and navigate around welds up to ½ inch in height was
thoroughly tested. The new aluminum chassis and treaded wheels were evaluated to ensure
reliable performance on warped liner bottoms and slope surfaces within the annulus space. The
differential drive control system was fine-tuned to optimize the crawler’s responsiveness and
stability during operation, and the crawler successfully demonstrated 180° maneuverability
within the confined 30" wide space.

3.2 Control System Validation and Performance
The VCS’s control system, comprising a newly developed control box, custom PCBs, and
ROS2-based architecture, underwent thorough testing to evaluate its responsiveness, reliability,
and operational precision. The system demonstrated excellent responsiveness to operator inputs,
with ROS2 nodes reliably managing camera feeds and teleoperation functions. Real-time video
feedback with low latency was validated, ensuring effective remote operation and precise
crawler navigation within the confined annulus space. The physical setup of the control box
connected to the crawler during testing is shown in Figure 13. illustrating the integration if
hardware components used to validate system performance. The Elimination of traditional
analog circuits and joystick interfaces marked significant improvement in system simplicity and
stability. The advanced controller streamlined operation and enhanced user control, allowing
operators to maintain crawler stability during complex maneuvers. Overall, the control system’s
performance confirms that the current design meets the fundamental requirements for DST
annulus cleaning. Insights gained from this comprehensive analysis will guide further
refinement of the system, focusing on optimizing vacuum efficiency and ensuring the crawler's
durability and reliability in the nuclear environment.

                                   4 CONCLUSION
The development and testing of the vacuum crawler system during this internship successfully
demonstrated its capability to address the critical challenges associated with the inspection and
cleaning of the annulus space in Double-Shell Tanks (DSTs) at DOE's Hanford site. The crawler's
robust new aluminum chassis ensured reliable maneuverability, and the new ROS2-based control
system provided precise operation with low-latency video feedback, contributing to the overall
effectiveness of the system. The crawler successfully performed its primary function of debris
removal, thus validating its design and operational capabilities.

The success of these initial testing trials indicates that the vacuum crawler system (VCS) is well-
positioned to advance into further stages of development, with the potential to significantly
enhance the safety and efficiency of hazardous waste management in nuclear facilities. Insights
gained from these trials will guide the next steps in refining the system's design. Planned
improvements for future iterations include:

   •   Full integration of the sensor suite with obstacle avoidance capabilities.
   •   Deployment of a comprehensive Graphical User Interface (GUI) for operator monitoring.
   •   Continued suction efficiency testing with various debris types, including cold spray
       powder.
   •   Radiation-hardening of components to achieve full in-tank readiness.
   •   Achieving Technology Readiness Level (TRL) 6 with a final CTF test and validation.

Additional improvements will focus on addressing:

   •   Weight distribution by adding ballast to the front of the crawler to achieve a more balanced
       center of gravity.
   •   Implementing a fail-safe start/stop system to enhance operational safety.
   •   Investigating the use of larger wheels to further improve obstacle traversal capabilities.
   •   Employing a non-moving vacuum head to optimize debris collection efficiency.

This project has demonstrated the viability of using remotely operated robotic systems for complex
environmental management tasks, contributing to the broader mission of the U.S. Department of
Energy's environmental remediation efforts. With continued development, the VCS has the
capability to become a valuable tool in supporting the maintenance of nuclear storage facilities.
The System’s successful deployment, as shown in Figure 12, underscores its readiness for further
testing and integration into hazardous waste management operations.
