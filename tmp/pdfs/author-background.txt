                                     1.0 INTRODUCTION​


1.1 Institutional Framework and Strategic Alignment

During the summer of 2026, Juan J. Zapata, a master's student in mechanical engineering from
Florida International University (FIU), joined the Office of the Chief Technology Officer (CTO)
at Hanford Tank Waste Operations & Closure (H2C). This research and development initiative is
embedded within H2C's Tank and Pipeline Integrity (TAPI) organization, directly contributing to
the U.S. Department of Energy’s (DOE) overarching environmental remediation and waste
management goals. This engineering effort is executed in strict alignment with the strategic
objectives of the Technology and Innovation Roadmap, RPP-PLAN-43988 (Rev. 09). This
roadmap formally establishes the mandate for developing automated robotic platforms and
remote technologies           designed for         radiological      surveying, inspections,   and physical
manipulations, with the ultimate objective of minimizing human exposure to high-radiation
environments. The explicit operational criteria driving this deployment are detailed within the
Technology Element Description Summary (TEDS) MTW-89. This document highlights the
specific regulatory and technical requirements for the remote cleaning of concrete surfaces
treated with a Special Protective Coating (SPC). Under this framework, the core focus of the
current project is the systematic design, development, and refinement of a remotely operated
robotic vacuum crawler system tailored specifically to navigate, vacuum, and maintain the
complex internal geometries of the DST annulus space.



1.2 Overview of Hanford’s Tank Infrastructure and Maintenance Challenges

The Hanford Site’s 28 in-service underground Double-Shell Tanks (DSTs), constructed between
1968 and 1986, serve as critical infrastructure for the safe isolation and storage of high-level
radioactive and chemical waste. Ensuring the structural integrity of these containment systems
requires rigorous, routine maintenance and inspection protocols. A primary component of this
effort is the periodic assessment of tank wall thinning and degradation via ultrasonic testing
(UT), which must be performed on both the primary tank walls and the secondary tank bottoms.

1.3 Project Overview and Objectives

Entering its third consecutive year, this project focuses on the iterative optimization, technical
refinement, and software modernization of the existing Annulus Vacuum Crawler prototype.
Building upon empirical performance data, field testing, and engineering feedback gathered
during Year 2, the current phase transitions the platform from a proof-of-concept prototype into a
robust, deployment-ready operational system. The primary objective is to enhance both the
mechanical reliability and software architecture of the crawler to ensure seamless navigation and
debris extraction within the highly constrained and radiologically hazardous Double-Shell Tank
(DST) annulus space.

To achieve this goal, the project is structured around four core technical objectives:

    ●​ Mechanical Refinement and Prototype Optimization: Re-engineer and upgrade the
         physical crawler chassis based on Year 1 feedback to resolve operational bottlenecks.
         This includes optimizing the physical footprint for 12-to-24-inch riser deployment and
         upgrading the debris extraction interface.
    ●​ Software Modernization and ROS2 Migration: Overhaul the existing control software
         to migrate the crawler’s architecture to a Robot Operating System 2 (ROS2) framework.
         This upgrade ensures a modular, deterministic, and scalable software stack, providing
         improved real-time node communication, enhanced safety critical guardrails, and
         standardized message packages for remote operations.
    ●​ Optimization of Remotely Operated Control Systems: Refine remote telemetry, live
         sensor feedback, and operator control interfaces. By utilizing ROS2's improved
         middleware, the system will deliver lower-latency data feeds to operators executing
         precise cleaning maneuvers from outside the high-radiation boundary.
    ●​ Facilitation of Non-Destructive Testing (NDT): Ensure the optimized system reliably
         clears a path along the primary tank walls and secondary tank bottoms. By systematically
         removing legacy mechanical corrosion debris and fine cold spray residue, the crawler
         directly enables subsequent ultrasonic testing (UT) crawlers to achieve unobstructed
         acoustic coupling for mandatory wall-thinning assessments.

Ultimately, this third-year phase bridges the gap between initial prototyping and field
deployment—delivering a smarter, more ruggedized, ROS2-compatible robotic solution that
minimizes human radiation exposure and supports Hanford’s long-term environmental
remediation mission.

2.1 Deployment strategies

To ensure seamless integration with existing field procedures, the Year 3 operational phase
maintains the baseline deployment strategy established during the initial project cycle.
Engineering enhancements are designed to fit directly into the pre-existing workflow,
minimizing the need for operational retraining or changes to auxiliary equipment. Crucially, all
physical testing and validation are conducted at the Hanford Cold Test Facility (CTF). The CTF
provides a non-radiological, full-scale mockup environment that precisely replicates the
geometric and material constraints of the actual DST annulus space. This allows for rigorous,
risk-free validation of the mechanical upgrades and the new ROS2 software architecture prior to
actual tank deployment

2.2 Mechanical Design

Building upon empirical feedback from the Year 2 prototype, the mechanical architecture of the
crawler was systematically re-engineered to resolve physical bottlenecks, simplify field
maintenance, and optimize maneuverability within the tight constraints of the DST annulus. The
specific hardware upgrades and structural modifications executed during this phase include:

2.21 Dimensional Reduction and Center of Gravity (CoG) Optimization

Reduced the overall length of the crawler by from 27.5in to 22.5in and the overall diameter by
1.25 inches to facilitate smoother deployment through restrictive 12-inch risers. Additionally, the
Venturi vacuum system was relocated to the center of the chassis, shifting the center of gravity to
the exact middle of the platform to improve stability and traction on the curved annulus floor.

2.3 Vacuum System Design & Development

2.31 System Architecture and Series Configuration

The baseline design, which positioned one vacuum module on the crawler and a second on the
umbilical harness was superseded to eliminate pressure drops across the tether. The refined
architecture integrates two EXAIR Line Vac pneumatic vacuum modules mounted directly
onboard the crawler in a series configuration.




                 Figure 16. Sullair Air Compressor Used for Validation of the Vacuum Performance


This onboard integration was made possible by re-engineering the chassis to feature a custom
CNC-machined cage, which allows the debris collection bag to be mounted directly to the
platform. By positioning the modules in series and directly on the crawler, the system drastically
maximizes both airflow and pull force at the cleaning nozzle, utilizing the first module to
generate high primary suction and the second to accelerate the material through the exhaust.

2.32 Fluid Mechanics and Component Optimization
Pneumatic efficiency is highly sensitive to flow restrictions and pressure drops over distance. To
optimize the system's fluid dynamics, several critical hardware adjustments were implemented
based on empirical testing:
    ●​ Coupling and Fitting Upgrades: During development, the pneumatic coupling size was
         increased from 3/8 inch to 1/2 inch. This modification expanded the internal volume,
         minimizing the airflow restrictions and pressure losses commonly caused by standard,
         narrow-diameter quick-connectors.
    ●​ Hose Line Stratification: For operational demonstration, a 1-inch hose line was utilized
         for high-velocity material intake, which subsequently adapted to a 1/2-inch line feeding
         the crawler's pneumatic supply.
    ●​ Line Vac Operational Mechanics: The EXAIR modules operate by injecting compressed
         air through an inlet into an annular plenum chamber. The air is then throttled through
         directed nozzles into the throat, creating a venture-driven vacuum at the intake. Because
         the Line Vac features a large, smooth bore with no moving parts, it prevents clogging and
         provides maintenance-free conveyance of highly abrasive radiological waste over long
         vertical lines.

2.4 Electronics & Controls

To maintain operational continuity and hardware reliability during the Year 2 development cycle,
the core electronic component stack was preserved from the previous iteration. The system
continues to leverage a dual-controller architecture utilizing a Raspberry Pi as the high-level
computer, a Teensy microcontroller for low-level hardware abstraction, RoboClaw motor
controllers, and high-torque Pololu metal gearmotors.
Rather than altering this proven hardware foundation, the Year 2 engineering focus was strictly
directed toward overhauling the system's software architecture. The previous iteration's control
framework exhibited communication faults and node instability; therefore, this phase completely
modernized the software ecosystem by migrating to a robust, deterministic Robot Operating
System 2 (ROS2) environment while optimizing the custom physical control interface.




                                   Figure 18. Electronic system of the new vacuum


2.41 Hardware Architecture & Control Interface
Signal integrity and field serviceability are maintained through a custom-designed Printed
Circuit Board (PCB). This centralized PCB establishes clean, trace-routed connections between
the Raspberry Pi, Teensy, and RoboClaw motor controllers. By consolidating these components
onto a single board, the design eliminates erratic wiring harnesses, mitigates electromagnetic
interference (EMI), and enhances overall system stability within the electromagnetically noisy
environments of the tank infrastructure.

2.42 ROS2 Software Ecosystem & Node Architecture
The core software logic was entirely reconstructed using modular ROS2 nodes to eliminate
latency bottlenecks and communication dropouts. The new control stack utilizes a decentralized
topology where tasks are isolated into dedicated, predictable processes:
    ●​ Input Processing (joy and teleop_twist_joy nodes): Raw hardware inputs from the
         Logitech controller are captured by a standardized joy_node. These inputs are passed to a
         telemetry-handling node that translates button presses and analog stick deflections into
         precise linear and angular velocity commands (Geometry_Msgs/Twist).
    ●​ Low-Level Abstraction and Actuation: The velocity commands are ingested by the
         Raspberry Pi and communicated down to the Teensy microcontroller. The Teensy
         executes real-time motor control loops via the RoboClaw drivers to regulate the Pololu
         motors, while simultaneously commanding the pulse-width modulation (PWM) servos
         governing camera orientation.
    ●​ Dual-Camera Feed Management: A dedicated video streaming node manages the
         dual-camera configuration, compressing and publishing high-frame-rate visual data over
         the network to the operator's console without lagging the primary control loops.
    ●​ Unified Launch System: To streamline field initialization, a comprehensive ROS2 launch
         script was written. This single script handles node parameter configuration, establishes
         the Data Distribution Service (DDS) middleware layer, and activates all operational
         nodes concurrently, ensuring a reliable, plug-and-play startup sequence.

3.1 Deployment Testing

Deployment testing was conducted to evaluate the ability of the VCS to be inserted through the
restricted access geometry of a 12-inch riser and transitioned onto the annulus floor. A 12-inch
riser section was used to replicate the dimensional constraint encountered during field
deployment. The crawler was manually positioned and lowered through the riser by a field
operator familiar with the deployment procedures used for tank operations. This test provided an
operational assessment of the redesigned crawler in addition to the dimensional verification
performed during the development process.




                                        Figure 23. Deployment testing at CTF


Particular attention was given to the crawler's orientation during deployment. The previous
crawler configuration incorporated a relatively flat front face, which caused the system to
naturally rotate toward the wheel side as it passed through the riser and approached the annulus
floor. This behavior could complicate deployment and require additional operator intervention to
correctly orient the crawler. The VCS was redesigned to improve its deployment behavior and
facilitate a more controlled transition from vertical insertion to horizontal operation.

3.11 Deployment Testing Result

The redesigned crawler was successfully deployed through the 12-inch riser without significant
difficulty. During the test, the crawler exhibited improved orientation behavior and transitioned
more predictably toward the wheel-side position upon reaching the deployment surface.
Following the test, the field operator evaluated the deployment process and indicated that the
redesigned VCS was easier to deploy compared with the previous year's crawler configuration.
The operator feedback provided additional validation that the mechanical modifications
improved the practical deployment procedure and reduced the complexity associated with
positioning the crawler during insertion.

                                             4.0 CONCLUSION

The third-year development of the Vacuum Crawler System (VCS) resulted in significant
improvements to the crawler's mechanical configuration, deployment characteristics,
debris-removal capability, and software architecture. The redesigned platform was developed to
address limitations identified during previous project iterations, with particular emphasis on
operation within the highly constrained geometry of the Double-Shell Tank (DST) annulus.

Functional testing at the Cold Test Facility (CTF) demonstrated that the redesigned crawler could
be successfully deployed through a 12-inch riser and operated within a simulated 32-inch-wide
annulus environment. Deployment testing also demonstrated improved handling and orientation
during riser insertion, with field-operator feedback indicating that the new configuration was
easier to deploy than the previous year's design. Maneuverability testing further demonstrated the
crawler's ability to perform directional turns and negotiate representative obstacles within the
confined annulus environment.

The integrated vacuum system was also successfully demonstrated during surface-cleaning tests.
The crawler was able to operate the onboard vacuum system and remove representative debris
from the test surface, validating the functionality of the revised vacuum configuration. In
parallel, the migration to a ROS2-based control architecture was verified using ROS2
visualization and diagnostic tools, including rqt and rqt_graph. The successful
communication between the system's software components confirmed the functionality of the
updated control architecture.

Overall, the results demonstrate that the VCS has progressed beyond the limitations identified in
previous iterations and now provides a more compact, maneuverable, and integrated platform for
remote debris-removal operations. The combination of mechanical refinement, onboard vacuum
integration, and ROS2-based control establishes a strong foundation for continued testing and
optimization. Further development will focus on improving the crawler's reliability,
debris-removal versatility, obstacle negotiation, and sensing capabilities in preparation for
increasingly representative operational testing.

                                           5.0 FUTURE WORK

Based on the feedback from operators and mentors, future development of the Vacuum Crawler
System (VCS) will focus on improving its maneuverability, durability, vacuum-system
versatility, obstacle avoidance, and environmental perception. The following areas have been
identified as priorities for continued development:

    ●​ Wheel Profile and Traction Optimization: Continue evaluating different wheel
         profiles, tread geometries, materials, and wheel combinations to improve traction, surface
         engagement, stability, and maneuverability within the confined and curved DST annulus
         environment. Testing will focus on identifying the configuration that provides the best
         balance between grip, obstacle clearance, and turning performance.
    ●​ Upward-Facing Vacuum Connection: Redesign the vacuum hose interface so that the
         connection between the hose and the onboard vacuum system is oriented upward. This
         configuration is expected to improve hose routing, reduce interference with the crawler
         and surrounding structures, and provide a more suitable connection arrangement for
         deployment and operation within the confined annulus.
    ●​ Manufacturing and Abrasion Protection of 3D-Printed Components: Transition
         critical 3D-printed components from prototype fabrication toward more durable
         manufacturing methods and materials. Components exposed to continuous debris contact
         or abrasive operating conditions will also be evaluated for the application of protective
         coatings to improve wear resistance and service life.
    ●​ Vacuum Head and Collection-Bag Optimization: Develop and evaluate different
         combinations of vacuum-head geometries and debris-collection bags to accommodate
         varying debris types and particle sizes. This modular approach would allow the VCS to
         be configured according to the characteristics of the material encountered during a
         specific cleaning operation.
    ●​ Vacuum Head Retention: Improve the mechanical attachment method used to secure the
         3D-printed vacuum head to the crawler. A more robust retention mechanism will be
         investigated to prevent the vacuum head from becoming detached during operation,
         particularly when the crawler encounters obstacles or uneven surfaces.

    ●​ Adjustable Vacuum Head: Investigate the development of a vertically adjustable
         vacuum head capable of moving upward and downward relative to the crawler chassis.
         This mechanism could improve obstacle avoidance while allowing the vacuum head to
         remain close to the cleaning surface during debris-removal operations.
    ●​ LiDAR Integration: Incorporate the LiDAR package developed and tested as part of the
         related sensing project into the VCS platform. The integration of LiDAR with the

         systems represents an important step toward a more capable and potentially
         semi-autonomous robotic platform.

capable of performing reliable debris-removal operations within the constrained DST annulus
