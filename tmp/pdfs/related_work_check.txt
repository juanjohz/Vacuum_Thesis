                                INTRODUCTION


1.1     Background
The Hanford Site was established in 1943 in southeastern Washington State as part
of the Manhattan Project, the United States’ wartime program to develop atomic
weapons. Its primary purpose was the large-scale production of plutonium through
the irradiation of uranium fuel and subsequent chemical separation. The location
offered several advantages for this mission, including a large area isolated from major
population centers, access to Columbia River water for reactor cooling, and an abundant
supply of hydroelectric power. These conditions supported the rapid construction of
nuclear reactors and processing facilities that became central to the nation’s nuclear
weapons program [2].




                     Figure 1.1: Aerial view of the Hanford Site


       Plutonium produced at Hanford was used in the Trinity test and in the atomic
bomb dropped on Nagasaki in 1945. Following World War II, production expanded
to meet the demands of the Cold War, extending the site’s operations for several
decades. Consequently, Hanford’s waste inventory developed from both its initial

                                          1
Manhattan Project activities and its subsequent defense production mission. In 1989,
the site’s mission shifted toward environmental cleanup, requiring the management
of the radioactive and chemical waste accumulated throughout its operating history
[3, 4].
          The recovery of plutonium from irradiated uranium fuel generated substantial
quantities of radioactive waste. Chemical processing separated the desired plutonium
from the fuel, leaving waste streams containing radioactive by-products and chemicals
used during separation. Much of this waste was transferred to underground storage
tanks arranged in groups known as tank farms within Hanford’s 200 Area. Over
time, the stored material developed into a complex mixture of liquid supernatant,
settled sludge, and saltcake. These forms differ in composition and physical behavior,
complicating their storage, characterization, and eventual retrieval [5, 6].
          The environmental consequences of this production legacy extend beyond
the waste retained in the tanks. Historical waste disposal practices and losses of
containment contributed to contamination of the surrounding soil and groundwater.
Tank leakage presents an additional concern because contaminants released into the
soil can migrate through the subsurface and potentially reach groundwater connected
to the Columbia River.       The rate and extent of this movement depend on the
properties of the contaminants and the surrounding geological conditions. Preventing
further releases is therefore essential to protecting water resources, ecosystems, and
nearby communities [6, 7].




                                           2
            Figure 1.2: Construction of underground tanks in Handford


       Secure storage is a fundamental requirement for managing this waste while
retrieval, treatment, and disposal activities proceed. Storage systems must maintain
containment over extended periods while allowing operators to monitor their condition
and identify deterioration. At Hanford, this requirement is particularly challenging
because much of the original infrastructure has remained in service beyond its intended
design life. Maintaining tank integrity therefore requires continued assessment of the
storage structures and the development of reliable methods for detecting conditions
that could compromise containment [8].
       The earliest underground storage system consisted of single-shell tanks (SSTs),
of which 149 were constructed between 1943 and 1964. These tanks incorporated a
carbon-steel liner encased in concrete, with the steel providing the primary barrier
between the waste and the surrounding structure. Their intended service life was
approximately 20 years. However, the prolonged storage of waste and the deterioration


                                          3
of aging tanks exposed the limitations of this configuration. Because SSTs lack a
separate secondary steel containment barrier, a failure of the liner can allow waste to
escape into the surrounding environment [8].
       To improve containment and support continued waste management, Hanford
constructed 28 double-shell tanks (DSTs) between 1968 and 1986. Each DST consists
of a primary carbon-steel tank positioned within a secondary carbon-steel liner and
surrounded by a reinforced-concrete structure.      The secondary liner provides an
additional barrier to retain waste if the primary tank leaks.       An approximately
30-inch-wide space, known as the annulus, separates the primary tank wall from
the secondary liner and provides access for monitoring and inspection. These tanks
receive waste retrieved from SSTs and support the transfer of waste for treatment.
Their continued operation depends on maintaining the integrity of both containment
barriers, making inspection and maintenance within the annular space an important
component of Hanford’s waste storage mission [9].




                   Figure 1.3: Ilustration of a Double Shell Tank

                                          4
1.2     Operational Context and Motivation

The continued use of Hanford’s double-shell tanks depends on periodic inspection
to assess the condition of their steel containment barriers. As these structures age,
corrosion and localized degradation can reduce the remaining metal thickness and
compromise their ability to retain waste. Visual examinations and ultrasonic inspections
provide complementary information for evaluating tank condition and planning maintenance.
While visual inspections document accessible surface conditions, thickness measurements
provide quantitative evidence of metal loss that may not be apparent from images
alone. Maintaining access to the surfaces being inspected is therefore an essential
part of the tank integrity program [9, 10].




                       Figure 1.4: Ultrasonic Testing Crawler


       Ultrasonic testing (UT) is a nondestructive inspection method used to evaluate

                                          5
material condition without removing a sample from the structure. In ultrasonic
thickness measurements, a transducer introduces high-frequency sound into the steel,
and the instrument determines thickness from the travel time of the returning echoes
and the calibrated sound velocity in the material. These measurements allow engineers
to assess remaining wall thickness and identify areas of thinning associated with
corrosion [11]. At Hanford, remotely operated inspection systems have been used to
examine DST walls, and specialized equipment has been developed to obtain thickness
measurements beneath the primary tank through air channels in its supporting insulating
pad. These applications demonstrate both the importance of UT and the access
challenges involved in inspecting underground tank structures [10].
       Debris accumulation within the annulus creates an additional obstacle to
inspection. The cleaning application addressed in this thesis includes loose corrosion
products and material released during mechanical surface preparation that can collect
on the annulus floor. Such deposits can obstruct inspection routes and limit access to
measurement locations. Where loose material remains between a contact transducer
and the steel, it can also interfere with acoustic coupling [12]. These considerations
make debris removal an important preparatory operation for inspecting affected areas.




                                          6
                        Figure 1.5: Debri Inside of the Annulus


       Tank refurbishment activities introduce a further cleaning requirement. Cold
spray technology deposits metal powder at supersonic speed to form a bonded layer on
a target surface. Hanford has applied this approach remotely to create a metal patch
on the floor between the primary and secondary tank walls as part of efforts to extend
tank service life [13]. For the cleaning application considered in this thesis, fine powder
residue associated with cold-spray operations is an additional target material. The
cleaning system must therefore address both larger loose debris and fine particulate
deposits in support of subsequent inspection and maintenance.
       The effect of this material on UT extends beyond physical access. Contact
ultrasonic measurements require effective acoustic coupling between the transducer
and the steel surface. Loose rust, scale, and dirt can interfere with the transmission of
sound into the material, while poor coupling can reduce the strength of the returning
signal and compromise measurement reliability [12]. Where loose powder or debris



                                            7
covers a required measurement location, surface cleaning may therefore be necessary
before a usable thickness reading can be obtained. Removing these deposits helps
establish suitable conditions for inspection, although the adequacy of the prepared
surface must still be assessed using the applicable UT procedure.
       These conditions motivate the development of a remotely operated system
for removing dust and debris from the DST annulus. The annulus provides limited
access to the tank surfaces, and Hanford already uses remotely operated equipment
for inspection and cold-spray application [9, 10, 13]. The cleaning approach considered
here likewise uses remote operation to address the confined geometry and keep personnel
outside the tank. A robotic vacuum crawler offers a means of moving an extraction
inlet to affected areas while allowing an operator to guide the system from outside
the tank. The objective is to collect loose material, clear inspection routes, and
improve access to surfaces required for subsequent ultrasonic examinations. This
operational need provides the motivation for the mechanical, vacuum, and control-
system development investigated in this thesis.

2.1      Related Work

2.1.1     FIU 2024: Pedro Chaviano’s Vacuum Crawler

Pedro Chaviano’s 2024 internship work investigated a remotely operated vacuum
crawler for cleaning the DST annulus. The prototype combined a mobile platform,
pneumatic debris collection, and camera feedback, establishing a starting point for
subsequent FIU development [16].
        Chaviano’s prototype used a three-dimensionally printed plastic chassis, four-
wheel differential drive, and two EXAIR Line Vac modules arranged in series. One
module was mounted on the crawler and the other along the umbilical, with a separate
debris collector. Analog controls and onboard cameras supported remote operation.
The report records debris-removal results ranging from 22% to 95% across the tested
configurations [16]. These results demonstrate collection capability but also show that
the best result cannot represent every hose arrangement. Remaining work included a
metal chassis, further mobility testing with its added mass, and additional cleaning
and sensing functions.
        This version established the feasibility of combining remote mobility with
pneumatic cleaning. Its prototype construction and configuration-dependent results
motivated continued development of the chassis, collection arrangement, and
operating controls. These earlier contributions form part of the project’s inherited
foundation.


                                          12
2.1.2     FIU 2025: Ronald Verde’s Vacuum Crawler

Ronald Verde continued the crawler’s development during 2025.              His version
introduced an aluminum chassis, treaded aluminum wheels, and an onboard collection
bag, with both pneumatic modules located on the crawler. The report also describes
a redesigned intake, deployment through mockup risers, and turning within a 30-inch
annulus representation [17]. These changes developed the mechanical platform and
brought the collection components together on the vehicle.
        Verde identified weight distribution, obstacle traversal, suction testing, and a
fail-safe start/stop function as areas for further development. Radiation hardening
and a final CTF validation were also listed as future work [17]. The report therefore
leaves further work before the configuration can be treated as a qualified tank-cleaning
system. In particular, the present project requires evaluation of the complete vehicle
with its hose attached and with representative obstacles and debris.
        The author implemented the Robot Operating System 2 (ROS 2) architecture
used in the present crawler as part of this thesis. Verde’s mechanical and pneumatic
work provides an inherited platform for that implementation and the subsequent
system evaluation described in Chapter 3.


2.1.3     Rolls-Royce Annulus Cleaning System

Rolls-Royce developed a robotic cleaning system for debris that obstructed inspection
and level monitoring in the Hanford tank annulus. The technology roadmap describes
mechanically relocating debris or removing it through containers. Following factory
testing, the system was demonstrated to Hanford personnel in Richland in 2019, and
operators received training [1]. Figure 2.1 shows the reported mockup.




                                           13
 Figure 2.1: Annulus floor cleaner mockup reported for the Rolls-Royce system [1].


        The roadmap marks the activity as complete and retired, and reports
successful factory acceptance testing. It does not identify a failure or explain why
the activity was retired. Consequently, retirement alone cannot support a claim that
the cleaner failed [1].
        Its relevance is the combination of remote mobility and debris handling
for the same operating space. However, the supplied summary gives no recovery
fraction, particle-size range, riser clearance, or obstacle-crossing results. It therefore
cannot establish compliance with this project’s 12-inch access and 0.75-inch obstacle
requirements.    For comparison, its principal limitation is the lack of published
performance detail in the available source; the vacuum crawler developed here
requires explicit tests of those functions.


2.1.4     Other Relevant Cleaning and Inspection Robots

The iRobot Roomba Combo j9+ provides a commercial example of an integrated
mobile vacuum cleaner. Intended for household floors, it combines suction with rubber
cleaning brushes, an edge-sweeping brush, and a retractable mop. Its navigation
system recognizes common household obstacles and uses room maps to organize
cleaning. The robot also returns to a dock for charging, debris emptying, and water
replenishment [18]. These functions illustrate how mobility, debris pickup, sensing,
and collection can be coordinated within one cleaning system.


                                              14
        For this project, its most relevant ideas are controlled coverage, obstacle
awareness, and coordinated servicing of the collection system. Such functions could
inform future operator assistance or cleaning-route planning. They would still need to
be adapted to the annulus geometry and validated with the target debris; household
cleaning performance does not demonstrate performance on corrosion products or
cold-spray residue.
        The physical configuration also limits direct use. The manufacturer lists a
robot footprint of 13.7 by 13.7 inches, exceeding the project’s nominal 12-inch riser
diameter [19]. Its dependence on a reachable dock for routine servicing would require
a different deployment and recovery arrangement in the tank. Furthermore, the
manufacturer’s obstacle-avoidance description does not establish the ability to cross
the specified protective pipes. The reviewed product documentation provides no
qualification for radiation exposure or contaminated-debris collection [18, 19].
        The Roomba is therefore a useful reference for integrating cleaning and
navigation, but its documented configuration is unsuitable for direct adoption as
this annulus crawler.    The thesis system must prioritize restricted access, hose
management, controlled retrieval, and verified debris recovery while retaining
operator visibility throughout the task.
