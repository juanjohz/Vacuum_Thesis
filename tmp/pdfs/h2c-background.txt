1.1 Pressure reference and location matter

Unless otherwise stated, pressure values in this report are gauge pressures relative to the local atmosphere:

A negative gauge reading indicates suction. A positive supply pressure of 100 PSIG does not imply a
vacuum of 100 PSI at the pickup.
The location of SP remains relevant. If it is near the Line Vac inlet and downstream of restrictive
components, it can closely represent the delivered motive pressure. If it is upstream of the regulator,
coupling, or branch restrictions, it measures the upstream condition. This qualification does not imply
that another permanent sensor must be purchased; first establish where the existing pressure tap is
located.

2 Suitability of the photographed pressure sensors

2.1 The visible range does not establish vacuum capability

The label on the photographed transmitter appears to read:

• pressure range: 0–150 PSI;

• electrical output: 0.5–4.5 V;

• supply: 5 VDC.

The manufacturer and exact part number are not legible enough to verify a datasheet. Assuming this is
a conventional positive gauge-pressure transmitter, it is unsuitable for calibrated vacuum measurement
at S2 . If the actual datasheet specifies a different pressure reference or a calibrated negative-pressure
range, that information would change the assessment.
A transmitter may survive a vacuum without accurately measuring it. A negative value calculated in
software can also arise from zero offset, noise, or extrapolation below the specified range. Therefore, use
a sensor explicitly specified for vacuum, compound gauge pressure, or a suitable differential-pressure
range.

   Specifications of the unshown sensor are separate

   The 0–150 PSI, 0.5–4.5 V, and 5 VDC markings above refer to the photographed transmitter type.
   The available information does not establish that the existing supply-line sensor SP is the same
   model. Retain SP and verify its own datasheet and calibration.


2.2 Why the range at S1 also needs attention

S1 is downstream of the ejectors. With a collection bag attached, its most useful role is tracking discharge
resistance. A 0–150 PSI range is much wider than the small positive pressures of interest in this channel.
If S1 remains upstream of bends and hose, its reading includes the effect of those downstream components
as well as the bag. It is not automatically the pressure drop across the bag alone. To measure a filter’s
pressure drop specifically, measure on the two sides of that filter.

2.3 Proposed measuring ranges

Select the calibrated measuring range separately from the permissible overload or proof-pressure rating.
A low-range sensor may have a high survival rating, but that does not extend its calibrated range.

3 One Line Vac versus two closely coupled units

3.1 What the manufacturer guidance supports

EXAIR states that two Line Vacs can improve conveying performance, but they do not double performance.
Its guidance identifies the greatest benefit in longer conveying runs: a second unit can be located farther
downstream to maintain particle velocity, including near a sweeping bend. It recommends spacing
multiple units along the conveying path instead of grouping them together [1].
The two units in the supplied apparatus are close together. This arrangement may help, but the
photographs cannot determine the additional pickup airflow, debris collection rate, or air consumption
benefit. It should be compared with a correctly assembled single-unit arrangement.
Engineering recommendation: for a short pickup-to-bag application, first establish a good single-Line-
Vac baseline. Keep the second unit if measured collection improvement justifies the added compressed-air
demand and collector airflow.

3.2 Suction pressure is not a complete performance measurement

Debris collection depends on air entering the nozzle fast enough to entrain particles and maintaining
sufficient transport through the hose.
An open pickup can move substantial air while showing modest negative pressure. Conversely, blocking
the pickup can produce a more negative reading even though no pickup air or debris enters. A pressure
reading alone cannot distinguish all useful and restricted-flow conditions.

3.4 Use the existing SP to assess the 100 PSI supply

EXAIR’s aluminum Line Vac instructions cover normal shop-air supplies up to 100 PSIG and recommend
checking pressure at the compressed-air inlet when flow or vacuum decreases [2].
Record the existing SP readings with one unit operating and then with both operating. A no-flow
pressure reading alone does not establish continuous air-delivery capability.
Interpret the result according to the actual tap location. If SP is upstream of a restriction, a steady
reading does not rule out pressure loss downstream. If needed, verify the pressure drop locally with
suitable available instrumentation before deciding that more permanent sensors are required.
The photographs show a common supply manifold and a quick disconnect. Their presence warrants
investigation, not a conclusion that they are undersized. EXAIR cautions that restrictive quick-connect
and push-lock fittings can reduce performance [4]. The compressor, regulator, manifold, branches, and
fittings must pass the required continuous air volume.


4 Collector, plumbing, and pressure-tap improvements

4.1 The collection bag must vent the conveying air

The bag is on the ejector discharge side. It receives the air drawn through the pickup plus the air injected
by every active Line Vac. Conservation of air mass gives

                                   ṁair,out = ṁair,pickup + ṁair,V1 + ṁair,V2 .                              (3)

The term for the removed unit disappears in the single-unit arrangement. Volumetric flows can be added
only after expressing them at the same reference pressure and temperature.
The engineering implication is that the second unit increases the airflow the bag must release, even if its
pickup benefit is small. A restrictive collector can reduce the useful gain.
EXAIR describes Line Vacs as discharging toward ambient conditions and cautions against opposing
discharge pressure and undersized exhaust piping [5]. Use a collector with sufficient breathable filtration
area, an adequately sized inlet, and filtration suitable for the actual particle size.
A sealed plastic bag alone does not provide the required air outlet. It can serve as a liner within a
suitably vented receiver, provided a filtered exhaust path remains. EXAIR documents a single-Line-Vac
pellet-cleanup application using a large collection sack with a planned filtration path for the outgoing
air [6]. This supports the architecture without establishing the appropriate model for the user’s debris.

4.2 Changes to prioritize in the photographed assembly

 1. Simplify the outlet turn. Test a straighter outlet or a larger-radius bend in place of the nearly
    180-degree turn immediately after V1 . The existing geometry presents turning losses and possible
    debris-accumulation locations; the benefit of changing it should be measured.

 2. Preserve adequate discharge passage. Avoid reducing the discharge to fit a small bag collar.
    Match the hose and connections to the manufacturer’s specified conveyance size.

 3. Keep the active Line Vac near the pickup. Use a short suction section and put most transport
    distance downstream. EXAIR favors mounting near the suction point and minimizing bends [2].

 4. Use a smooth nozzle transition. Provide a passage appropriate for the largest expected debris
    and a controlled air-entry gap when collecting from a surface. Hold these dimensions fixed in the
    comparison test.

 5. Retain the existing supply-pressure instrumentation. Keep SP assigned to Psupply . Improve
    the local taps for S1 and S2 as needed without moving them onto the compressed-air supply line.

4.4 Pressure taps and debris protection

The existing S1 and S2 taps are located on bends. For more representative static-pressure measurements,
use a straight suction section between the nozzle and the active Line Vac, and a straight discharge
section toward the collector.
Use flush, burr-free sidewall openings perpendicular to flow rather than probes projecting into the stream.
DwyerOmega explains that turbulence, aspiration, and impingement at a static tap can materially affect
readings [7].

• Provide each selected sensor’s required supply voltage.

• Use an appropriate current-input interface for 4–20 mA models.

• Scale voltage outputs to the permitted analog-input range.

• Verify the exact connector pinout, process thread, and mating cable.

• Update pressure conversion, zero, full-scale value, and output direction in software.

A controller with a 3.3 V analog input requires suitable conditioning for 0–5 V or 0–10 V signals.
Pressure-range compatibility alone does not establish electrical compatibility.

7 Recommended revised configuration and remaining inputs
For the first revision, use the following arrangement:

 1. Retain the existing supply sensor SP . Verify its specifications and tapping location, and log it
    with the Line Vac operating.

 2. Establish a one-Line-Vac baseline near the pickup. Use a smooth, adequately sized discharge
    route and compare it with the tandem assembly.

 3. Use a vacuum-capable instrument at S2 . Select the ProSense PTD25-20-VH for economical
    monitoring, the EPS25-V14-1001 for a local display and diagnostics, or the Omega PX309-015V5V
    when better stated static accuracy is the priority.

 4. Use a low-pressure instrument at S1 . The ProSense PTD25-20-0100WCH is a practical initial
    candidate for discharge monitoring.

 5. Use an adequately vented collection bag or filtered receiver. Size its flow passage and
    filtration area for all conveying air, and evaluate the effect of filter loading.

present, so neither photographed transmitter needs to be reassigned to that role. The assessment of the
vacuum and discharge channels remains separate.

compressed-air flow, hose lengths and diameters, nozzle dimensions, lift and bends, collector/filter
resistance, debris size and density, and required collection rate. The exact model and location of SP
would also make its operating data easier to interpret.

8 Research literature and the master’s thesis opportunity

8.1 Research motivation and defensible knowledge gap

The apparatus provides a tractable mechanical-engineering research platform because ejector performance,
nozzle entrainment, pneumatic transport, and collector resistance are coupled. The main engineering
question is not simply which configuration gives the most negative value at S2 . It is which configuration
collects a defined mass of the intended debris, at a specified recovery requirement, with acceptable
compressed-air consumption and repeatability. The compressed-air sensor SP is already installed (though
absent from the supplied figure); it should be logged throughout the proposed studies. The pressure
range and location of SP must be verified before using its reading as the delivered ejector motive pressure.
The papers summarized below establish that commercial ejector performance, gas–solid entrainment,
multiple air-amplifier arrangements, transport losses, filtration and control have each been investigated.
Consequently, neither “using an ejector to vacuum powder” nor “putting two ejectors in series” is a
sufficient novelty claim. A thesis contribution must instead specify a new, reproducible operating
envelope, predictive model, design rule, diagnostic method, or experimentally validated
control improvement for the selected debris and geometry. The exact EXAIR Line Vac model and
the intended debris distribution remain to be confirmed; results from other devices cannot be directly
transferred without validation.

9 Five candidate mechanical-engineering master’s thesis topics
Each of the following is a separate potential thesis, rather than a set of five required research tasks.
Topic A is the most direct use of the existing equipment. Topic C is a focused experimental alternative.
Topic E is most appropriate after an operating map and repeatable pickup have been established. These
proposed hypotheses require testing; they are not findings.

9.1 Topic A — Single versus tandem Line Vacs: recovery and specific air use

Proposed title: Experimental Characterization and Compressed-Air-Use Optimization of Single and
Tandem Ejectors for Small-Debris Collection.
Research question. Under what motive-pressure, nozzle and downstream-resistance conditions does
operating both nearby Line Vacs increase actual debris recovery enough to justify the additional
compressed-air requirement?
Testable hypothesis. A tandem arrangement may increase debris capture or transport under some
conditions, but the benefit will depend on collector resistance and available motive flow. When the two
units are close together and the collector resistance is substantial, a properly configured single unit may
have a lower specific air requirement at equal acceptable recovery.
Independent variables. Ejector configuration (one or two); feasible delivered motive-pressure level;
and controlled collector-resistance state. A secondary study could vary inter-ejector separation or
discharge-bend radius, but this should not be added until the primary experiment is complete. The
same material and nozzle configuration must be held constant across paired trials.
Method. Establish a true single-unit configuration by replacing the removed unit with a smooth
connector of appropriate bore; do not simply disable one ejector and leave its internal passage installed.
Maintain matched hose length, outlet connection, nozzle height and debris presentation as closely as
possible, documenting any unavoidable change. Compare:

 1. Equal delivered motive pressure at each active unit, where the air system can maintain that
    condition. This tests the practical benefit of adding a unit at a common pressure, but the total
    motive air use need not match.

 2. Equal total motive-air flow or air budget, over the feasible overlapping range. This tests
    whether the tandem arrangement provides superior collection with the same air expenditure, and
    may require independent regulators/valves and calibrated motive-air flow measurement.

Report cases that cannot maintain the target pressure as supply-limited results rather than silently
treating them as pressure-matched trials.
Measurements. Use SP under flow; measure S2 and S1 with appropriately ranged instruments; weigh
the debris before and after each trial; measure time, motive air consumed, and clean-air pickup flow
where practical. For flow-based comparisons, verify the delivered pressure at the inlet of each active
ejector or characterize restrictions between SP and those inlets.
Analysis and contribution. Construct collection-rate, recovery-fraction, and specific air-consumption
response surfaces with uncertainty intervals. Identify a practical operating envelope in which the second
ejector is beneficial, neutral or detrimental. A negative result demonstrating the superiority of one
ejector within defined conditions is a valid and publishable design finding. Published double-Venturi and

serial-amplifier results motivate the comparison but do not predict it [18, 19, 20].
Feasible master’s scope. One debris surrogate, one nozzle, two configurations, three pressure levels
and three collector states for the main matrix; reserve spacing/geometry changes for a bounded follow-on
study.

9.2 Topic B — Nozzle geometry, pickup gap, and validated entrainment modeling

Proposed title: Design and Experimental Validation of Pickup-Nozzle Geometry for an Ejector-Driven
Debris Collector.
Research question. How do nozzle entrance geometry, effective throat area and distance from the
surface affect recovery, clean-air entrainment, and deposition for a fixed air budget?
Testable hypothesis. An inlet transition optimized for the chosen particle-size distribution and a
controlled stand-off distance can improve recovery without increasing motive-air consumption. An overly
restrictive nozzle may make S2 more negative while decreasing useful pickup airflow.
Proposed hardware. Fabricate a small set of interchangeable nozzle inserts: a plain circular opening,
a radiused/bell-mouth opening, and a rectangular slot. Compare geometries at matched open area where
feasible; retain the same downstream system and one Line Vac. A simple adjustable fixture should
maintain stand-off height, yaw and pitch relative to the debris surface.
Measurements and model. Obtain recovery, collection rate, S2 , motive flow and inlet air velocity or
clean-air induced volume flow for each trial. Develop a clean-air CFD model of the nozzle/suction section
with a measured downstream pressure/flow boundary condition. Validate the model against measured
pressure and flow over an operating range; only then assess whether a limited Eulerian–Lagrangian or
CFD–DEM particle model is needed. Mesh independence and sensitivity to turbulence closure should be
reported, not assumed.
Contribution. A validated geometry recommendation and a dimensionless design map using stand-off-
to-nozzle size, entrance area, and particle-size-to-opening ratios. Relevant nozzle and gas–solid transport
precedents are available [25, 20, 21]. Do not claim that these precedents establish the optimum for H2C.
Feasible master’s scope. Three nozzles, two stand-off distances and three feasible pressure settings,
with replicated debris tests and a clean-air CFD model. Full multiphase CFD–DEM is optional, not a
prerequisite.

9.3 Topic C — Filter loading, discharge resistance, and service criterion

Proposed title: Experimental Modeling of Collector-Loading Effects on Pickup Performance in a
Pneumatic Debris Collection System.
Research question. How does accumulated material alter a collector’s air resistance and reduce recovery,
and can a measured pressure-difference trend define a reliable servicing or replacement condition?
Testable hypothesis. At controlled motive conditions and nozzle geometry, increasing collector
resistance reduces induced suction airflow and eventually reduces debris recovery. Effective media area
and loading history modify the shape of that degradation curve.
Independent variables. Effective filter-media area (e.g., two verified areas using the same medium),
mass loading or standardized resistance state, and a modest set of operating pressures. Keep the material


Pressure sensing and pneumatic conveying                                                           17 of 26
H2C Vacuum Debris Collection                                                 Assessment and Thesis Planning

and particle-size distribution fixed during model development.
Measurement boundary. Distinguish S1 (discharge-side pressure at its tap) from filter pressure drop,
which requires pressure measurements immediately upstream and downstream of the media. If an added
differential sensor is used, verify its static pressure and dust-protection ratings. Determine filter face
velocity from the total actual volumetric airflow at the filter inlet divided by active filtration area; this
airflow includes entrained pickup air and the ejectors’ motive air. Different flow quantities must not be
mixed without converting them to a common state.
Experimental method. Begin with a clean, unladen medium. Add standardized portions of debris,
record the filter pressure difference, SP , S1 , S2 , collection performance and air consumption, then repeat
across independent loading cycles. Treat checkpoints inside one loading cycle as correlated repeated
measures, not independent replicates. Model the dependence of resistance on loading and face velocity
and test a withheld loading cycle.
Contribution. A validated collector-pressure-loss model and a service or filter-area criterion tied to
a specified recovery threshold, with uncertainty. Bag-filter cake research demonstrates that filtration
velocity can change cake resistance, but a small unpulsed bag may behave differently [22].
Feasible master’s scope. Two media areas, a single debris material, one nozzle and one ejector, with
multiple full loading cycles. This offers a strong thesis without needing to optimize internal ejector
geometry.

9.4 Topic D — Fault diagnosis from pressure, airflow, and recovery

Proposed title: Experimental Development of Pressure and Airflow Diagnostics for Faults in an
Ejector-Driven Debris Collector.
Research question. Can a small, physically interpretable sensor set distinguish normal collection,
partial inlet obstruction, an intake-hose leak and increased collector resistance?
Testable hypothesis. Joint features from SP , S2 , S1 and an independent flow/recovery reference can
distinguish at least some faults more reliably than an absolute threshold applied only to the suction
pressure.
Fault protocol. Impose a calibrated set of restrictions, controlled leak openings and repeatable
filter-resistance states. Record ground-truth conditions from the imposed fault fixture plus weighing and
flow measurement. Include healthy runs at different motive-pressure settings, because an operating-point
change should not be misclassified as a fault.
Data and inference. Start with mean gauge pressures, slopes, pressure ratios and run-to-run variance.
Add band-limited transient features only after checking the sensor response time and sampling rate.
Compare interpretable thresholds or a state machine with one bounded classifier, and evaluate a confusion
matrix, false alarms and detection latency on entire runs or days withheld from training. In particular,
do not randomly mix time windows from the same run between training and test sets.
Contribution. A sensor-necessity assessment and reproducible diagnostic decision rules. Dense-phase
blockage work motivates pressure-signal features, but the flow regime is different and published feature
thresholds are not directly transferable [23].
Feasible master’s scope. Four operating states (healthy plus three faults), two pressure levels, two
debris-loading conditions, and repeated labeled runs. A complex neural-network model is unnecessary

9.5 Topic E — Feedback regulation and reduced compressed-air consumption

Proposed title: Feedback Regulation of Motive Pressure for Reduced Compressed-Air Consumption in
Pneumatic Debris Collection.
Research question. Can automatic adjustment of motive pressure preserve a prescribed collection
performance while reducing the standard volume of compressed air used per mass recovered as the
collector becomes more restrictive?
Testable hypothesis. A sensor-driven pressure schedule with a bounded PI correction may reduce air
use relative to a conservative high-pressure setting, and in some conditions relative to a fixed pressure
selected for a clean collector. Whether it outperforms the best feasible constant-pressure setting must be
established experimentally.
Actuation and model. Use a rated controllable regulator with known command/fail behavior. Perform
steady tests at several pressure setpoints and resistance states to estimate a static map from measured
delivered pressure to induced airflow, recovery and motive-air consumption. With one regulator feeding
a fixed Line Vac, motive pressure and flow are coupled; do not claim independent control of both unless
separate actuators exist.
Control design and comparison. Start with an open-loop pressure schedule; implement bounded
PI feedback with anti-windup, filtered measurements, and safe pressure limits only after verifying
actuator authority and response speed. Compare (i) a feasible conservative high fixed pressure, (ii)
the best constant pressure found during characterization, and (iii) the scheduled/feedback approach on
comparable debris and collector-loading sequences.
Outcomes and contribution. Report recovery constraints, air consumed per kilogram, collection time,
steady-state pressure error and robustness to loading. Electrical energy reduction must not be claimed
from air-volume savings alone; it requires measured compressor electric power or a validated, explicitly
bounded compressor model. Research on pressure/airflow selection for other pneumatic conveyors
provides a method precedent rather than direct H2C controller gains [24, 28].
Feasible master’s scope. One ejector, one nozzle, three resistance states, a mapped regulator and one
practical control method. More advanced MPC is optional after the basic schedule and PI comparisons
are established.
