LIGHT DETECTION AND RANGING (LIDAR) BACKPACK DEVELOPMENT
    FOR AUTOMATED SALT CLOSURE MONITORING AT THE WASTE
                           ISOLATION PILOT PLANT

                                         by
                                 Benjamin Vargas
                       Florida International University, 2026

                                  Miami, Florida
                     Professor Leonel Lagos, Major Professor

The Waste Isolation Pilot Plant (WIPP) is the United States’ only deep geological
repository for defense-related transuranic waste and requires continuous monitoring

of tunnel deformation caused by salt creep. Traditional instruments provide limited
spatial coverage, motivating a mobile, high-fidelity mapping approach. This research
develops a wearable Light Detection and Ranging (LIDAR) based system to generate

accurate three-dimensional representations of underground corridors.
   The system integrates an Ouster OS1 LIDAR, four calibrated cameras, and
Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) within the Robot Operating Sys-

tem 2 (ROS2). Rigid-body transformations and intrinsic camera modeling project
three-dimensional LIDAR points into image space, producing geometrically consis-
tent colorized point clouds. The lightweight frame supports stable, operator-based

deployment in GPS-denied environments.
   Experimental validation demonstrates metrically consistent reconstructions suit-

able for temporal alignment and differential analysis. The resulting maps support
quantitative change detection to measure salt closure over time, providing a scalable
monitoring approach aligned with Department of Energy operational objectives at

WIPP.

                               INTRODUCTION


1.1    Background

The enduring management of radioactive waste constitutes one of the most per-

sistent challenges linked to nuclear technology. In the United States, substantial
amounts of radioactive waste were produced during World War II and the Cold War
due to nuclear weapons research, development, and production initiatives. These

efforts led to the generation of transuranic (TRU) waste, principally composed of
materials contaminated with long-lived alpha-emitting radionuclides, including plu-
tonium [8]. The primary goal of the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) has been to

guarantee the secure separation of nuclear waste from the biosphere across geological
durations.
   The Waste Isolation Pilot Plant (WIPP), situated in Carlsbad, New Mexico, was

created to tackle this issue and functions as the nation’s sole deep geological repos-
itory for defense-related transuranic waste. Sanctioned by Congress via the Waste
Isolation Pilot Plant Land Withdrawal Act, WIPP commenced disposal operations

in 1999 and receives waste produced by DOE facilities around the United States
[3]. The facility is essential to the national nuclear cleanup initiative by offering
a permanent disposal solution that mitigates environmental hazards and long-term

management obligations.
   WIPP is situated roughly 650 meters beneath the surface in the Salado For-
mation, a substantial salt deposit chosen for its advantageous geomechanical and

hydrological characteristics (as shown in Figure 1.1). Rock salt demonstrates min-
imal permeability, lacks moving groundwater, and undergoes plastic deformation
under prolonged stress. This plastic deformation induces the progressive creep of



                                          1
salt, leading to the closure of cracks, voids, and excavated apertures, a phenomenon

generally known as natural closure [9]. As disposal chambers undergo deformation
over time, the adjacent salt encases the waste, offering a passive and self-sealing
isolation mechanism independent of active engineered systems.




            Figure 1.1: Overview of the Waste Isolation Pilot Plant [1].


   The long-term safety approach at WIPP depends on the synergistic performance
of manmade barriers and natural geological processes, as illustrated in Figure 1.2.

Waste containers, backfill materials, and repository design elements are enhanced
by the inherent creep characteristics of salt, which gradually diminishes empty space
and constrains possible routes for radioactive migration [10]. Performance evalua-

tions conducted by the Department of Energy and assessed by the U.S. Environ-
mental Protection Agency, as shown in Figure 1.3, analyze repository functionality
over regulatory durations exceeding 10,000 years.

                      Figure 1.2: Shipment of TRU waste [2].




                  Figure 1.3: WIPP undergrounds operations [3].


   Besides its disposal function, WIPP possesses national importance as a singular

operational demonstration of deep geological disposal for radioactive waste in the
United States. The facility facilitates remediation initiatives at several historical
DOE locations and supplies essential data for comprehending long-term repository




                                         3
dynamics. Ongoing surveillance of subterranean conditions, particularly tunnel de-

formation due to salt creep, is crucial for maintaining operating safety, adhering to
regulations, and verifying long-term performance.
   The subterranean conditions of WIPP impose distinctive operational limitations,

such as restricted access, changing tunnel configurations, and diminished vision.
These reasons drive the advancement of sophisticated sensing and mapping systems
that can precisely delineate subsurface geometry in areas devoid of GPS. High-

fidelity spatial data are essential for quantifying deformation trends, facilitating
maintenance planning, and improving situational awareness, hence directly motivat-
ing the development of the Light Detection and Ranging (LIDAR)-based mapping

system in this research.



1.2    Motivation

Comprehending and measuring the rate of salt creep is essential for the secure

operation and long-term performance evaluation of WIPP. In salt formations, the
ongoing viscoplastic deformation of halite dictates the sealing of excavated voids, the
development of porosity and permeability, and finally the entrapment of deposited

waste [11].
   Salt creep affects the design and predictive models employed in performance
evaluations by regulating the rate of shrinkage in mined areas and the healing of

fractures. Preliminary studies at WIPP revealed that the measured closure rates
underground were markedly more rapid than initial laboratory predictions, prompt-
ing an ongoing research initiative to align field observations with laboratory results

and enhance constitutive models of salt deformation [11]. Precise characterization of




                                          4
creep rate is essential to guarantee that models accurately predict repository closure

behavior throughout operational and regulatory timeframes.
   Operationally, salt creep impacts ground control in subterranean settings. The
gradual yet continuous deformation of the ceiling, ribs, and floor of access drifts

necessitates regular maintenance, fastening, and monitoring to provide safe working
conditions and safeguard installed infrastructure [12]. Should creep rates surpass
projections, ground support systems must adjust to mitigate any undue deformation

that may endanger workers or equipment.




                     Figure 1.4: Salt convergence at WIPP [4].


   The capacity of salt to flow and self-seal is fundamental to the safety argument of
WIPP from a performance assessment standpoint. Salt creep gradually diminishes

void space and compacts crushed salt backfill, steering the repository towards a low-
permeability condition that restricts fluid travel paths [13]. Creep-induced closure,
as seen in Figure 1.4, directly affects essential repository performance indicators,




                                          5
such as brine transport, alterations in disturbed rock zone characteristics, and the

development of permeability surrounding excavations [14].
   Measuring salt creep rates also guides engineering decisions about trash em-
placement sequencing and retrievability. Accelerated closure rates may need more

frequent scheduling of garbage insertion and backfilling activities to maintain ac-
cessibility of apertures during operations. Conversely, comprehending long-term
creep behavior facilitates the planning for post-closure performance and regulatory-

required predictions of radioactive containment over millennia. These considerations
collectively designate the assessment of salt creep rates as a pragmatic and scientific
priority for WIPP. Precise creep metrics enhance model accuracy, bolster operational

safety, and strengthen the enduring isolation properties essential for the repository’s
regulatory adherence and national objectives.



1.3    WIPP Constraints and Requirements

The subterranean conditions at WIPP impose a series of physical, operational, and
legal limitations that directly affect the viability of deformation monitoring equip-
ment. Access to disposal panels and service drifts is constrained both spatially and

temporally because of ongoing waste emplacement, ground control operations, and
safety regulations regarding worker exposure [3]. As salt creep gradually modifies
tunnel geometry, monitoring systems must function inside constantly evolving cross-

sections and in restricted regions where fixed instrumentation coverage is necessarily
constrained.
   Visibility conditions in the subterranean environment are difficult and fluctuate

with operational activities. The illumination is artificial and uneven, dust may
accumulate from vehicular activity or maintenance tasks, and surfaces frequently




                                          6
appear optically uniform, as illustrated in Figure 1.5. These variables diminish

the efficacy of conventional optical surveying methods and hinder feature-based
measures that depend on significant visual contrast. The subterranean environment
is entirely devoid of GPS, necessitating the use of local coordinate systems and

internally coherent localization techniques for all spatial data references.




            Figure 1.5: Example of homogeneous surfaces at WIPP [5].


   From an operational perspective, monitoring technology must reduce disrup-

tion to standard repository operations. Stationary convergence monitoring devices,
however efficient at specific sites, offer restricted spatial resolution and necessitate
installation, upkeep, and regular recalibration [12]. These constraints elevate labor

needs and constrain the capacity to swiftly characterize deformation throughout
extensive tunnel networks. Consequently, there is a want for mobile sensing tech-
nologies that can obtain spatially continuous measurements without the need for

permanent installation.




                                           7
   Technologies utilized underground must be resilient to severe environmental con-

ditions as illustrated in Figure 1.6. Equipment must withstand high humidity, salt
dust, vibrations from vehicular traffic, and prolonged operation in confined environ-
ments. Power availability is limited, encouraging devices with minimal energy use

or battery-operated functionality. Data acquisition systems must function indepen-
dently due to the constraints of wireless transmission capacity and the assumption
of continuous distant access being unreliable.




                   Figure 1.6: Robust environment at WIPP [6].


   To adequately facilitate salt creep monitoring at WIPP, a sensing platform must
fulfill certain essential criteria. The system must deliver centimeter-level geometric

precision across extended corridor distances to identify gradual deformation trends.
It must operate dependably in GPS-denied contexts and sustain localization con-
sistency despite restricted geometric features. The platform must be portable and

swiftly deployable to decrease operating overhead and mitigate disruption to subter-
ranean activities. The system must produce consistent, high-fidelity spatial datasets




                                          8
that facilitate quantitative comparisons over time, allowing for the identification of

subtle convergence rates rather than only qualitative observations.
   These constraints jointly drive the creation of a transportable, LIDAR-based
mapping device proficient in generating dense three-dimensional representations of

subterranean geometry. This technology enhances current fixed equipment by of-
fering geographical context, broadening coverage over entire drifts and panels, and
facilitating deformation evaluation at a scale aligned with the operational and safety

requirements of WIPP.

                             LITERATURE REVIEW



2.1     Current Mapping Technologies

Subterranean facilities are increasingly relying on 3D spatial data for inspection,
documentation, and change detection in areas without GPS. Today’s technologies at
WIPP include (1) static laser scanning and classic surveying procedures, (2) mobile

scanning equipment for walk-through or vehicle data collection, and (3) image-
based reconstruction methods for building 3D models from photographic data. The
primary purpose of these methods is to offer metrically consistent geometry that

enables temporal comparison and quantification of deformation, clearance changes,
and infrastructure status.



2.1.1    Static Laser Scanning

Terrestrial static laser scanning is a popular reference technique for producing high-
precision as-built documentation in tunnels, drifts, and other constrained environ-

ments; however, it often necessitates multiple scanning installations, line-of-sight
considerations, and time-consuming field operations. This minimizes the frequency
of static searches in big networks, particularly when access times are limited or

neighboring activity must continue. Figure 2.1 demonstrates that static scanning
may achieve low centimeter-level errors under controlled procedures, making it suit-

density and noise patterns during a scan in subterranean environments.

2.4    Simultaneous Localization and Mapping

When GPS is not available, SLAM computes the sensor trajectory and generates a
coherent 3D map. The most typical failure mechanism in subterranean tunnels is the

accumulation of drift caused by minute frame-to-frame changes. Loop closure and
global optimization (shown in Figure 2.5) minimize drift when the platform encoun-
ters previously mapped regions, which is crucial for repeat mapping for temporal

change detection [31, 32].

2.5     LIDAR-Camera Sensor Fusion

LIDAR and camera systems offer complementary sensing capabilities. LIDAR uses
active ranging to directly measure metric depth, resulting in three-dimensional point



                                          28
clouds that are geometrically correct. Cameras gather passive radiometric data,

which gives detailed color and texture information but lacks intrinsic depth with-
out multi-view reconstruction. Sensor fusion integrates many modalities to create
spatially coherent geometry enhanced with visual information.

   This fusion is especially effective in subterranean environments. LIDAR main-
tains geometric coherence in GPS-denied areas, whereas cameras improve inter-
pretability by identifying material limits, structural features, and surface conditions

that geometry alone cannot reveal. The integrated system makes it easier to track
quantitative deformation and do qualitative inspections.

2.5.4     Benefits and Limitations in Underground Environments

LIDAR-camera fusion has a variety of applications in WIPP-like corridors. It pro-

motes the visual study of geological features and material demarcations, improves
flaw detection in walls, ceilings, and support structures, and enables more intuitive
comparisons between successive surveys. Fusion, which adds color data to geo-

metrically coherent point clouds, facilitates quantitative deformation analysis and
qualitative inspection in massive subterranean tunnels.
   Nonetheless, limitations exist. Uniform salt surfaces typically have poor visual

texture, limiting the efficiency of image-based analysis. Artificial illumination causes
varied exposure and shadowing effects, which may impact color consistency among
surveys. Dust and particulate matter reduce optical performance, lowering return

strength and image quality. Furthermore, temporal misalignment of sensors may
result in spatial aberrations in the integrated point cloud. These restrictions em-
phasize the significance of solid extrinsic calibration, dependable synchronization,

and regular data-gathering processes for reproducible and interpretable outcomes.



2.5.5     Role in Salt Closure Monitoring

In deformation monitoring, geometry is the fundamental measurement variable.
Color does not directly improve range precision, but it does help with interpretability
and validation. Colorized geometry enables faster visual verification of convergence

zones, localized spalling, and surface changes while calculating cross-sectional dif-
ferences between temporally separated scans.




                                          32
   As a result, this work employs LIDAR-camera fusion as an interpretability layer,

together with a geometrically coherent SLAM-derived map. The fusion technique
maintains metric precision while boosting situational awareness and facilitating the
visualization of long corridor segments.

   The sensor fusion architecture intends to augment the reconstructed geometry
with radiometric data from RGB cameras while keeping the metrics of LIDAR-based

SLAM intact. The fusion technique is deliberately modular. A projection-based aug-
mentation layer adds color, while LIDAR-centric SLAM guarantees that the shapes
remain consistent. This disaggregation ensures that deformation measurements are

never based on anything except active ranging data. On the other hand, visual
information makes it easier to examine and comprehend.
   The fusion pipeline is built on the ROS2 middleware framework and consists of

four concurrent processes: the Ouster driver, which sends point clouds; the USB
camera nodes, which send synchronized image streams; the static transform broad-
caster, which defines the rigid transform tree; and the custom colorization node,

which converts three-dimensional LIDAR points into image dimensions. The tech-
nique’s modular design enables live and offline rosbag playback. Data from sub-
surface demonstrations at WIPP is frequently recorded first and then processed to

reduce processing time during collection.

4.3    Projection-Based Colorization Algorithm

The study foregoes the tightly coupled visual-LIDAR SLAM strategy in favor of a

projection-based fusion method. The speed with which projection-based colorization
is completed has little effect on the accuracy of geometric forecasts. Large-scale
mapping procedures, where geometry is crucial and color assists comprehension,

commonly utilize equivalent methods [42, 43].
   The custom ROS2 node is updated by the picture topic for each camera and the
LIDAR point cloud subject. In response to a point cloud message, the node fetches

the relevant static transform from TF2, converts each point into a potential camera
frame, projects the point using the intrinsic model, and samples the related RGB
value. The result is delivered as a PointCloud2 message with colors.

   The projection loop is depicted here in simplified pseudocode.

for point in pointcloud:

      p_base = transform_lidar_to_base(point)
      for cam in cameras:
          p_cam = transform_base_to_camera(p_base, cam)

          if p_cam.z > 0:
                u, v = project_to_image(p_cam, cam.K)
                if pixel_in_bounds(u, v):

                   rgb = image[v, u]
                   assign_color(point, rgb)
                   break

   To guarantee the geometric backbone remains stable prior to radiometric as-
signment, this modular method allows for colorization based on replay after SLAM
optimization.

comparing two surveys performed at times t1 and t2 , using the formula


                                                            
                           ∆(x, t1 , t2 ) = d M(t1 ), M(t2 ) ,                   (5.4)

     where d(·) represents the distance between clouds or surfaces. The internal
stitching of the surveys into a single continuous frame allows for differentiation
across entire drift extents rather than just limited local locations.

     This method allows you to generate a permanent master map from a series
of WIPP scans. By comparing new surveys to this worldwide norm, we may de-
termine whether there has been any change over time. Unlike fixed convergence

devices, which measure displacement between discrete anchor points, this approach



                                           69
captures continuous geometric progression throughout entire drift cross-sections and

longitudinal distances.
   Adding color to these stitched corridor models makes them more understandable.
Engineers can employ radiometric context to link convergence zones to structural

elements, installed hardware, or geologic boundaries, whereas geometry is used to
determine the extent of displacement. Without affecting the metric basis of analy-
sis, deviation maps layered over colored geometry provide rapid spatial context for

observed change.
   The potential operational basis for a future WIPP demonstration may involve
combining multi-epoch registration and SLAM-based corridor stitching. Rather

than quantifying salt creep as separate sites, sequential scans can be incorporated
into unified corridor models to directly compare repeated traversals.

                                 CONCLUSION



6.1    Summary of Objectives and Contributions

The primary aim of this thesis was to illustrate the viability of employing repeated
three-dimensional mapping to identify deformation caused by salt creep in under-
ground passageways at WIPP. The primary concept was that geometric differencing

may identify quantifiable closure tendencies, contingent upon the generation of spa-
tially consistent maps across many temporal epochs and their alignment within a
stable coordinate framework.

   This thesis presents a comprehensive end-to-end workflow, encompassing the col-
lection of mobile LIDAR data and the identification of changes between point clouds.
The pipeline incorporates hardware design, data acquisition using ROS2, SLAM

with LIDAR, projection-based colorization, and subsequent analysis in CloudCom-
pare. The objective of each design step was to maintain the metric’s validity and
enable more spatial comparisons.

   The LIDAR backpack is compliant with WIPP operational criteria. The created
mounts maintain the close interactions of all the sensors. Simultaneous logging with
both LIDAR and cameras in a GPS-denied environment is now feasible due to the

Jetson-based computing architecture. The system may be effortlessly relocated,
quickly configured, and utilized repeatedly for corridor surveys.
   Rather than perceiving SLAM as a method to augment accuracy, individuals re-

garded it as a tool to improve visibility. LIDAR-centric SLAM offers the necessary
geometric framework for comparing findings across various time intervals by sustain-

ing a consistent trajectory and reducing drift. The principal reason for alterations




                                         71
in deformation estimates, as opposed to physical changes, would be algorithmic

aberrations due to the lack of continuous registration.
   We employed a Trimble X7 terrestrial laser scanner to perform controlled millimeter-
scale experiments for evaluating the change-detection toolchain. The findings of the

paper-stack experiment indicated that displacements analogous to the documented
rates of salt convergence can be identified using the post-processing pipeline. This
illustrates that the analytical method can identify shape alterations that are crucial

for repository-level monitoring.
   Finally, metric independence is preserved by the modular LIDAR-camera fusion
architecture, which incorporates radiometric context into geometry. While coloriza-

tion improves reading and increases environmental awareness, it does not influence
the deformation metric. This division asserts that active ranging data is the exclu-
sive foundation for quantitative results.



6.2    Implications for Salt Closure Monitoring at WIPP

Currently, WIPP is meticulously monitoring geotechnical conditions with instru-
mented, point-based measurements, including extensometers and convergence me-

ters. Despite their ease of installation and maintenance, these systems have limited
coverage and produce a significant amount of relevant data.
   The solution presented in this thesis enhances existing technologies by ensur-

ing spatial consistency over entire drifts. Dispersed deformation patterns are un-
detectable with a limited number of measurements; nevertheless, mobile LIDAR
mapping can identify them. By repeatedly creating these maps, one may witness

convergence across the entire surface and track temporal changes. This methodology
offers color-coded maps for enhanced visibility, quick deployment without permanent




                                            72
installation, consistent geometry for quantitative differentiation, and corridor-scale

geographical coverage.


   • Corridor-scale spatial coverage,

   • Rapid deployment without permanent installation,

   • Repeatable geometry suitable for quantitative differencing,

   • Visual interpretability through colorized maps.


   Transitioning from sparse point measurements to distributed surface fields en-
hances the monitoring paradigm from discrete convergence readings to comprehen-
sive surface deformation analysis. This facilitates improved ground control planning,

prioritization of maintenance, and evaluation of repository engineering tasks.



6.3    Limitations

We must acknowledge specific constraints. Instead of employing the wearable back-
pack, a terrestrial scanner was utilized for millimeter-scale validation. Nonetheless,
this does not yet assess the field accuracy of the mobile system; it merely delineates

the functionalities of the post-processing technique.
   Furthermore, SLAM drift and registration quality continue to affect cross-epoch
alignment. Estimates of deformation magnitude can be influenced by minor pose

variations in extensive passages with limited characteristics. Verifying registrations
and diligently resolving outstanding issues are essential.
   Thirdly, the analysis of dense point clouds is protracted due to the sequential

operation of the existing projection-based colorization node across points and cam-
eras. While the implementation is functional, it requires optimization for large-scale
application.



                                         73
   A fourth issue is that, although the technical architecture is presented in this

thesis, longitudinal datasets collected directly at WIPP over prolonged durations
are still absent. Ongoing surveys performed under actual repository settings are
essential for comprehensive operational validation.



6.4     Future Work

Future study should concentrate on computational optimization, accurate charac-
terization of wearable platforms, and subsurface demonstration at WIPP.



6.4.1    Optimization of the Colorization Node

The current implementation processes each point sequentially and performs per-
point TF lookups, transformations, projections, and RGB packing operations [?].

This architecture, while functional, results in increased processing time that es-
calates linearly with the number of points and cameras. It is advisable to focus
optimization in three principal domains.


1. Elimination of Redundant Per-Point Operations For each prospective
point and camera, the existing system performs TF lookups and rigid transforma-
tions within the innermost loop. It is advisable to request transformations solely

once for each camera and point cloud, thereafter converting them into homoge-
neous matrices, as the extrinsic relationship between the LIDAR and each camera
remains constant throughout a callback. The complete point cloud can be altered

by replacing the per-point do transform point procedures with vectorized matrix
multiplication. This change alone substantially decreases the overhead of loops at
the Python level and access to TensorFlow buffers.




                                           74
2. Vectorization and GPU Acceleration Currently, each point sequentially

receives RGB assignment and projection. NumPy arrays facilitate the execution of
vectorized operations on point clouds, enabling tasks such as perspective division,
pixel projection, and batch processing. The intrinsic parallelism of transformation

and projection renders these processes advantageous for CUDA acceleration on the
Jetson Orin Nano. Enhancing the speed of dense Ouster scans and approaching
real-time performance can be accomplished by GPU-based matrix multiplication

and filtering.


3. Intelligent Camera Selection and Calibration Refinement The node
now identifies the initial legitimate projection following the examination of each

camera separately. Geometric pre-filtering based on the camera’s orientation is
employed to minimize superfluous projection efforts. Choosing the camera with
the minimal incidence angle, instead of the initial successful match, can enhance

color constancy. The geometric framework employed for deformation analysis can
be maintained, while the reduction of reprojection error facilitates enhanced edge
alignment. This is because it can somewhat reduce the mechanical tolerances of

extrinsic parameters.
   By delineating geometry and color augmentation, these enhancements facilitate
system scalability, enhance radiometric consistency, and diminish resource require-

ments.
   Algorithmic acceleration will enhance offline processing speed, potentially en-
abling near real-time viewing during deployment. Maintaining the geometric preci-

sion necessary for deformation monitoring is achievable without subsampling when
handling denser point clouds, which is an additional advantage of minimizing per-
formance overhead.




                                        75
   Enhancing extrinsic calibration to reduce reprojection error could improve color

alignment accuracy, facilitating comprehension while preserving a clear distinction
between geometry and radiometry. These improvements will transform the node
from a proof-of-concept into a scalable fusion module appropriate for prolonged

subterranean application.



6.4.2    Demonstration at WIPP

The primary objective of this research is to apply it within the WIPP underground

environment. The subsequent elements must be incorporated into the demonstration
procedure:


   • Baseline corridor scanning,

   • Repeat surveys over defined time intervals,

   • Cross-epoch map alignment,

   • Quantitative deformation field extraction,

   • Comparison against existing convergence instrumentation.


   Integrating LIDAR deformation fields with sensor observations yields corrobora-

tion from many sources. The reliability of both types of monitoring would improve if
discrete convergence meters and distributed surface measurements were consistent.



6.5     Closing Remarks

Creating reproducible three-dimensional maps facilitates the identification of alter-

ations in subterranean conduits, as evidenced in this thesis. A unified framework for




                                         76
spatial salt closure monitoring has been established by the integration of LIDAR-

centric SLAM, rigid mechanical design, ROS2-based integration, and cloud-to-cloud
differencing.
   Over time, each survey serves as a geometric record of the repository’s state.

These recordings demonstrate deformation as measurable spatial fields instead of
distinct point values when analyzed over time. Should the wearable LIDAR back-
pack system undergo further refinement and field testing, it may augment our un-

derstanding of salt creep mechanisms, facilitate operational monitoring at WIPP,
and integrate with current geotechnical instruments.
