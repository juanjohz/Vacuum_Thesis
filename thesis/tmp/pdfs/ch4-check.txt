                                INTRODUCTION



1.1     Background
The Hanford Site was established in 1943 in southeastern Washington State as part
of the Manhattan Project, the United States’ wartime program to develop atomic
weapons. Its primary purpose was the large-scale production of plutonium through
the irradiation of uranium fuel and subsequent chemical separation. The location
offered several advantages for this mission, including a large area isolated from
major population centers, access to Columbia River water for reactor cooling, and
an abundant supply of hydroelectric power. These conditions supported the rapid
construction of nuclear reactors and processing facilities that became central to the
nation’s nuclear weapons program [1].




                      Figure 1.1: Aerial view of the Hanford Site


       Plutonium produced at Hanford was used in the Trinity test and in the atomic
bomb dropped on Nagasaki in 1945. Following World War II, production expanded
to meet the demands of the Cold War, extending the site’s operations for several
decades. Consequently, Hanford’s waste inventory developed from both its initial
Manhattan Project activities and its subsequent defense production mission. In 1989,

                                          1
the site’s mission shifted toward environmental cleanup, requiring the management
of the radioactive and chemical waste accumulated throughout its operating history
[2, 3].
          The recovery of plutonium from irradiated uranium fuel generated substantial
quantities of radioactive waste. Chemical processing separated the desired plutonium
from the fuel, leaving waste streams containing radioactive by-products and chemicals
used during separation. Much of this waste was transferred to underground storage
tanks arranged in groups known as tank farms within Hanford’s 200 Area. Over
time, the stored material developed into a complex mixture of liquid supernatant,
settled sludge, and saltcake. These forms differ in composition and physical behavior,
complicating their storage, characterization, and eventual retrieval [4, 5].
          The environmental consequences of this production legacy extend beyond
the waste retained in the tanks. Historical waste disposal practices and losses of
containment contributed to contamination of the surrounding soil and groundwater.
Tank leakage presents an additional concern because contaminants released into the
soil can migrate through the subsurface and potentially reach groundwater connected
to the Columbia River.       The rate and extent of this movement depend on the
properties of the contaminants and the surrounding geological conditions. Preventing
further releases is therefore essential to protecting water resources, ecosystems, and
nearby communities [5, 6].




                                           2
              Figure 1.2: Construction of underground tanks in Handford


      Secure storage is a fundamental requirement for managing this waste while
retrieval, treatment, and disposal activities proceed. Storage systems must maintain
containment over extended periods while allowing operators to monitor their
condition and identify deterioration. At Hanford, this requirement is particularly
challenging because much of the original infrastructure has remained in service
beyond its intended design life.     Maintaining tank integrity therefore requires
continued assessment of the storage structures and the development of reliable
methods for detecting conditions that could compromise containment [7].
      The earliest underground storage system consisted of single-shell tanks
(SSTs), of which 149 were constructed between 1943 and 1964.              These tanks
incorporated a carbon-steel liner encased in concrete, with the steel providing the
primary barrier between the waste and the surrounding structure. Their intended
service life was approximately 20 years. However, the prolonged storage of waste
and the deterioration of aging tanks exposed the limitations of this configuration.
Because SSTs lack a separate secondary steel containment barrier, a failure of the


                                         3
liner can allow waste to escape into the surrounding environment [7].
       To improve containment and support continued waste management, Hanford
constructed 28 double-shell tanks (DSTs) between 1968 and 1986. Each DST consists
of a primary carbon-steel tank positioned within a secondary carbon-steel liner and
surrounded by a reinforced-concrete structure.       The secondary liner provides an
additional barrier to retain waste if the primary tank leaks. The annulus separates
the primary tank wall from the secondary liner and provides access for monitoring
and inspection. These tanks receive waste retrieved from SSTs and support the
transfer of waste for treatment. Their continued operation depends on maintaining
the integrity of both containment barriers, making inspection and maintenance within
the annular space an important component of Hanford’s waste storage mission [8].
The annulus width specified for this project is 32 inches from wall to wall, as confirmed
by the author; this dimension does not account for the bottom curvature discussed
in Section 1.41.

1.2      Operational Context and Motivation
Hanford’s double-shell tanks are inspected regularly to check the condition of the steel
that contains the waste. As these structures age, corrosion and localized degradation
can reduce the remaining metal thickness and compromise their ability to retain waste.
Visual examinations and ultrasonic inspections provide complementary information
for evaluating tank condition and planning maintenance. While visual inspections
document accessible surface conditions, thickness measurements provide quantitative
evidence of metal loss that may not be apparent from images alone. Maintaining
access to the surfaces being inspected is therefore an essential part of the tank integrity
program [8, 9].




                          Figure 1.4: Ultrasonic Testing Crawler


       Ultrasonic testing (UT) is a nondestructive inspection method used to
evaluate material condition without removing a sample from the structure.               In
ultrasonic thickness measurements, a transducer introduces high-frequency sound
into the steel, and the instrument determines thickness from the travel time of

                                            5
the returning echoes and the calibrated sound velocity in the material.         These
measurements allow engineers to assess remaining wall thickness and identify areas
of thinning associated with corrosion [10]. At Hanford, remotely operated inspection
systems have been used to examine DST walls, and specialized equipment has been
developed to obtain thickness measurements beneath the primary tank through air
channels in its supporting insulating pad. These applications demonstrate both the
importance of UT and the access challenges involved in inspecting underground tank
structures [9].
       The cleaning application addressed in this thesis concerns loose debris,
including corrosion products and material released during mechanical surface
preparation, that can collect on the annulus floor. Accumulated debris may hinder
inspection equipment from reaching required measurement locations. Separately,
loose material on the steel surface being examined can interfere with the transmission
of ultrasonic waves between the probe and the steel. This transmission requires
effective acoustic coupling at the measurement location [11]. Debris on the annulus
floor does not necessarily affect measurements on tank walls. Vacuum cleaning may
support access and removal of loose material, but it does not by itself establish that
a measurement surface meets the preparation requirements of the applicable UT
procedure.




                                          6
                       Figure 1.5: Debri Inside of the Annulus


       These conditions motivate the development of a remotely operated system
for removing loose debris from the DST annulus. The annulus provides limited
access to the tank surfaces, and Hanford already uses remotely operated equipment
for inspection [8, 9]. The cleaning approach considered here likewise uses remote
operation to address the confined geometry and keep personnel outside the tank. A
robotic vacuum crawler offers a means of moving an extraction inlet to affected areas
while allowing an operator to guide the system from outside the tank. The objective
is to collect loose material from the annulus floor, with the intended benefit of
clearing inspection routes and improving equipment access to required measurement
locations. Testing will evaluate debris recovery and crawler operation; improved
UT measurements and compliance with UT surface-preparation requirements are not
claimed outcomes. This operational need provides the motivation for the mechanical,
vacuum, and control-system development investigated in this thesis.




                                         7
1.3      Research Objectives and Questions
The aim of this thesis is to develop and experimentally evaluate a remotely operated
vacuum crawler for removing loose debris from the annulus of Hanford double-shell
tanks. The primary research emphasis is the vacuuming performance and compressed-
air requirements of the debris-collection subsystem. The specific objectives are to:


  1. Characterize debris-collection performance under defined operating
      conditions using debris recovery efficiency, collection rate, and blockage
      occurrence.

  2. Evaluate vacuum-subsystem configurations and operating conditions
      to identify an arrangement that achieves acceptable debris recovery with
      reduced compressed-air consumption per unit mass collected. Assess the effects
      of delivered supply pressure and downstream collection resistance on these
      outcomes.

  3. Integrate the selected vacuum configuration with the crawler and
      Robot Operating System 2 (ROS 2) operator-control system, and
      evaluate debris collection and operator feedback during representative cleaning
      tasks.

  4. Verify    deployment,       mobility,     and   retrieval    capability    under
      representative access, clearance, and obstacle conditions with the operating
      hose and control connections attached.


       These objectives lead to four research questions:

  1. What debris recovery efficiency, collection rate, and blockage occurrence does
      the vacuum subsystem achieve for the defined debris and operating conditions?




                                          8
  2. How well does the vacuum system collect debris under different configurations,
       supply pressures, and collection resistances, and how much compressed air does
       it use?

  3. Can the integrated crawler perform representative cleaning tasks while
       providing the operator with sufficient control and visual feedback?

  4. Can the complete crawler be deployed, maneuvered, and retrieved within the
       specified geometric constraints with its hose and control connections attached?

        The experimental evaluation will establish performance limits and support
selection among the configurations tested. Acceptance criteria will be defined before
the main experiments. The findings will apply to the materials, configurations, and
operating conditions evaluated; they will not establish a universally optimal vacuum
system. Section 1.4 defines the application constraints. Chapter 3 describes the
selected implementation and distinguishes the author’s work from inherited features;
Chapter 4 defines the test conditions, metrics, and acceptance criteria.

1.41      Geometric and Access Constraints

The confirmed project annulus width is 32 inches from wall to wall. This dimension
does not account for the curvature at the tank bottom, which reduces the usable
clearance near the floor. The allowable vehicle envelope must therefore be checked

against the local tank profile. Figure 1.6 illustrates the curvature and piping but
provides no dimensional verification.
       The project uses a riser with a confirmed diameter of 12 inches. The allowable
deployment envelope must fit within that opening with clearance for insertion and
retrieval; the riser diameter is not an allowable 12-inch crawler diameter. The envelope
must include cameras, lights, fittings, hose connections, and cables in the deployment
configuration. Dimensional inspection will record the minimum clearance, and a
passage demonstration will verify insertion and retrieval without interference that
prevents deployment or damages the crawler.

1.42     Deployment and Retrieval Requirements

The crawler must be lowered, placed on the annulus floor, and retrieved with its hose
and cables attached without damage that compromises operation. It must support its
own assembled weight while suspended from the designated lifting attachment during
deployment and retrieval. Verification will record the assembled mass, suspension
configuration, and any hose or cable load carried by the attachment. Passing requires


                                           10
completion of lowering, placement, and retrieval without structural separation or
damage that prevents subsequent motion, collection, or operator feedback.



1.43    Mobility and Obstacle Traversal Requirements

The crawler must travel forward, reverse, turn within the available annulus clearance,
and cross protective pipes associated with thermocouple instrumentation while its
hose and cables remain attached. The protective pipes have a confirmed outside
diameter of 0.75 inch. The installed obstacle profile must represent the pipe and
its supports; outside diameter alone does not specify the height of an elevated pipe
above the floor. Passing requires completion of the specified maneuvers and crossing
without immobilization, overturning, or damage to instrumentation. Verification will
record completed maneuvers, interference, and failures under the representative pipe
placement and hose routing; travel distance, turn extent, and repetition count will be
defined in Chapter 4.



1.44    Debris Collection Requirements

The target materials are dust and sand-type debris representative of loose corrosion
products and material generated by mechanical surface preparation on the annulus
floor. Representative materials, particle-size distributions, moisture condition, and
test mixture remain to be specified for testing. Cold-spray residue is not a target
material.
       The system must pick up the specified debris, transport it through the
complete vacuum path, and retain it in the collection system.        Verification will
distinguish material left at the pickup area, material lodged in the transport
path, and material recovered in the collector. Proposed performance measures are
recovered mass divided by introduced mass, collection time, and blockage events



                                         11
per trial. The debris mixture, introduced mass, hose length and routing, operating
duration, and collection-system loading must be specified for each comparison.
Recovery, time, and blockage acceptance limits have not yet been established; no
numerical pass threshold is assigned here.


1.45     Operator Feedback and Control Requirements

Video feedback and onboard lighting must let the operator identify travel direction,
approaching obstacles, surrounding clearance, and debris at the pickup inlet during
navigation and cleaning.     Remote viewing is also identified in earlier Hanford
inspection-system requirements [12].     A proposed visibility criterion is that the
operator can identify designated clearance boundaries, the representative pipe, and
the inlet working area during forward travel, reversing, turning, and pickup. The
specific viewing targets and test lighting conditions require confirmation.
       End-to-end video latency is a proposed measurement, with no acceptance
limit yet set.   Stopping on command-link loss and requiring deliberate restart
remain proposed control requirements.        If adopted, verification will measure the
time and travel distance from link loss to stopping under specified speed and
hose-loading conditions, and check that communication recovery alone does not
restart motion. These behaviors and their thresholds must be approved and tested
before being reported as established capabilities. Chapter 3 will describe the selected
implementation, and Chapter 4 will specify the verification procedures.

2.1     Related Work

2.11    FIU 2024: Pedro Chaviano’s Vacuum Crawler

Pedro Chaviano’s 2024 internship work investigated a remotely operated vacuum
crawler for cleaning the DST annulus. The prototype combined a mobile platform
and pneumatic debris collection, establishing a starting point for subsequent FIU
development [13]. Figure 2.1 shows this prototype.
       Chaviano’s prototype used a three-dimensionally printed plastic chassis, four-
wheel differential drive, and a small EXAIR Venturi vacuum module approximately
1 in in diameter. It used analog control with a Raspberry Pi Pico and did not use
camera feedback [13]. Chaviano conducted debris-removal experiments; the reported
vacuuming efficiency from 22% to 95%. The prototype used a vacuum line rather
than an onboard collection bag, requiring debris to travel approximately 40ft to
the collection point. That long conveying path may have contributed to the low
measured efficiency, although the available report does not establish it as the cause.
The prototype was not robust, and its vacuuming properties were not characterized
sufficiently to establish reliable performance.   Remaining work included a metal
chassis, further mobility testing with its added mass, and additional cleaning and
sensing functions.




                                         13
          Figure 2.1: FIU 2024 vacuum crawler developed by Pedro Chaviano.
                         Project image supplied by the author.


       This version established the feasibility of combining remote mobility with
pneumatic cleaning. Its prototype construction and configuration-dependent results
motivated continued development of the chassis, collection arrangement, and
operating controls. These earlier contributions form part of the project’s inherited
foundation.



2.12     FIU 2025: Ronald Verde’s Vacuum Crawler

Ronald Verde continued the crawler’s development during 2025. His version used an
aluminum chassis, treaded aluminum wheels, and an onboard collection bag, with
both pneumatic modules located on the crawler [14]. These changes provided a
metal platform with the collection components integrated onto the vehicle, shown
in Figure 2.2.




                                        14
           Figure 2.2: FIU 2025 vacuum crawler developed by Ronald Verde.
                         Project image supplied by the author.


        Although the chassis was made from aluminum, the center of gravity was
shifted forward relative to the wheel placement. As observed by the author, the
crawler consequently tilted backward during operation, preventing the pickup inlet
from maintaining the position needed for vacuuming. The inherited crawler also
could not turn within the annulus or traverse the required obstacles. The aluminum
construction therefore did not resolve the practical cleaning and mobility requirements
of the project. These limitations motivate the present work on debris collection,
maneuverability, and obstacle traversal with the complete crawler and its attached
hose.



2.13     Rolls-Royce Annulus Cleaning System

Rolls-Royce developed a robotic cleaning system for debris that obstructed inspection
and level monitoring in the Hanford tank annulus. The technology roadmap describes
mechanically relocating debris or removing it through containers. Following factory
testing, the system was demonstrated to Hanford personnel in Richland in 2019, and

                                          15
operators received training [15]. Figure 2.3 shows the reported mockup.




         Figure 2.3: Annulus floor cleaner mockup reported for the Rolls-Royce
                                       system [15].


       The roadmap records successful factory acceptance testing and lists the
cleaning activity as complete and retired [15]. The available roadmap does not report
conduit-crossing performance or explain why the activity was retired. The pictured
wheel geometry appears to offer limited ability to cross raised conduits, but this
visual observation is not a demonstrated performance result. The system’s reported
configuration is more consistent with a floor-cleaning platform than a vacuum-based
debris-transport system; this distinction is relevant because the present project
requires debris to be conveyed into a collection system.
       The system demonstrates prior remote annulus-cleaning development. Its
retired status and the limits of what the available documentation establishes provide
context for the remaining project requirements: debris collection and transport, access
across the required obstacles, and retrieval.




                                          16
2.14     Summary of Existing Annulus Cleaning Technologies

The systems reviewed for this application comprise two FIU vacuum-crawler
prototypes and the Rolls-Royce annulus floor-cleaning system.         They represent
different approaches: pneumatic debris conveyance through a vacuum line, an
aluminum crawler with an onboard collection bag, and a remotely operated floor-
cleaning platform. The FIU prototypes provide project-specific evidence of attempted
vacuum collection, while the Rolls-Royce roadmap documents factory acceptance
testing, a 2019 demonstration, and eventual retirement of the activity [13–15].
       Each documented system has limitations against the present project’s
combined requirements.     Chaviano’s prototype had limited characterization and
robustness, and its long vacuum line may have reduced recovery. Verde’s crawler
did not maintain pickup orientation and could not turn or traverse the required
obstacles, based on the author’s observations of the inherited system. The Rolls-
Royce source does not report vacuum-based debris transport or conduit-crossing
results; its pictured geometry suggests possible access limitations, but this requires
operational evidence. The author reports that all three systems have since been
decommissioned. Their documented limitations mean they do not provide a currently

       The research need is therefore an integrated crawler that can be deployed

        Technologies
The annulus cleaner requires a source of suction and a way to retain the recovered
debris for later disposal, or to convey it to a disposal point. This section reviews
pneumatic conveyors, electric vacuum motors, and side-channel blowers, focusing
on how their airflow arrangements produce suction and transport air through the
collection path.



2.21     Compressed-Air Venturi Vacuum Systems

Pneumatic vacuum generators use compressed air to produce suction without a
rotating impeller. In a Venturi ejector, an accelerated air stream draws air through a
suction connection, and both streams leave through the exhaust. Compact ejectors
are widely used in vacuum handling, including gripping airtight workpieces [16]. For
debris collection, however, the device must also provide a passage suitable for the
material being transported; a suction-gripping ejector is not automatically a solids
conveyor.
       The EXAIR Line Vac is an example of an air-operated conveyor relevant to this
distinction. Directed compressed-air jets create suction at its intake and accelerate
material through an open bore into the discharge hose. The device has no moving
parts, and its conveying rate can be adjusted through the supply-pressure regulator
[17]. This arrangement is relevant to a compact crawler because the conveying module
can be located near the pickup point without an onboard vacuum motor.

2.22     Electric Motor-Driven Vacuum Systems

An electric vacuum motor rotates an impeller or fan to move air from the inlet toward
the exhaust. The moving air lowers pressure at the inlet, drawing air and entrained

                                           19
debris through the pickup nozzle and hose. In a through-flow motor, the working air
also passes through and cools the motor; in a bypass motor, a separate airflow cools
the motor while the working stream follows its own path through the vacuum unit
[20]. In either arrangement, the collector and filter sit in the working-air path and
must separate debris without restricting the flow excessively.
       Fan geometry and stage count determine the balance between airflow and
pressure rise: single-stage designs generally favor airflow at lower pressure differences,
while additional stages can increase pressure capability [20]. Airflow carries debris
through the line, while pressure must overcome losses at the pickup, hose, collector,
and filter. For the crawler, an electric source could remain outside the deployment
envelope, but its performance depends on the complete connected path. Testing
should therefore measure airflow and debris recovery with the intended nozzle,
hose, collector, and filter; motor rating alone does not establish pickup or transport
performance.

2.23     Regenerative or Side-Channel Blower Systems

A regenerative, or side-channel, blower also uses an electric motor and rotating
impeller, but moves air through a ring-shaped side channel. As the impeller turns,


                                           20
it repeatedly transfers energy to the gas circulating through the channel, producing
a pressure difference between the inlet and outlet. Repeated energy transfer allows
the blower to sustain suction or pressure without contact between the impeller and
housing [21]. Unlike the vacuum-motor arrangements above, the gas remains in the
blower’s channel as it gains energy; the connected hose, collector, and filter still
determine the airflow available at the pickup point.
       The blower moves air rather than separating debris, so a collector and filter
must retain solids upstream and protect its inlet. Resistance from the hose, loaded
filter, or blockage can reduce airflow and affect cooling; Busch consequently requires
suitable dust filtration, adequate cooling and minimum gas flow, and pressure relief
where the inlet may be closed [22]. For this project, its suitability as an external
source depends on airflow through the actual hose, separator, and filter. Continuous-
duty capability alone does not establish debris-conveying performance.

2.3      Crawler Mechanical Configurations and
         Mobility

2.30     Introduction

The crawler must carry the vacuum equipment and position the pickup while
moving through the annulus. The relevant mechanical choices concern the chassis
configuration, the locomotion system, and the compliance available to accommodate
changes in the floor profile. This section reviews these choices in relation to equipment
support, maneuverability, and passage over protective pipes.



2.31     Chassis Configurations

Chassis articulation and suspension describe related but distinct features. Articulation
allows connected frame sections or wheel-support assemblies to rotate relative to
one another.    Suspension allows wheel movement relative to the main body to
accommodate terrain and loading. The two can overlap: a jointed bogie can serve
as a suspension mechanism without springs, while a rigid main body can carry
independently suspended wheels. Here, a rigid chassis refers to a frame with fixed
wheel mounting positions and no suspension travel; tire deformation is considered
separately.
       A rigid chassis provides fixed attachment locations for the vacuum equipment
and pickup, without joints between frame sections or moving wheel supports. For
this project, that arrangement simplifies equipment mounting and keeps the relative
positions of the components fixed. Its limitation is ground accommodation. When the
four supporting points lie at unequal elevations, the frame cannot adjust individual
wheel positions, so wheel loading can become uneven and a wheel may lose contact.
Bouton and Gao’s analysis contrasts rigid four-point support with a passive bogie,



                                           23
which permits unequal wheel elevations while approximately equalizing the loads on
its two wheels [23].
       An articulated chassis can accommodate changes in terrain through its joints,
although the available motion depends on the joint axes. A steering joint alone
does not provide vertical wheel travel. In the MARCEL rover, a central steering
joint and a rear bogie provide distinct motions; active bogie torque also redistributes
wheel loading. The central joint occupies equipment space, while bogie placement
affects both payload accommodation and ground clearance [23].            For a vacuum
crawler, equipment and hoses spanning moving sections would consequently need to
accommodate that motion.
       A rigid main body with suspension offers another arrangement: the equipment
remains on a common frame while the wheel supports move. Passive suspension
accommodates terrain through its mechanical response, whereas active suspension
adds controlled adjustment of wheel position or loading.            These mechanisms
can improve wheel contact, but add joints and supporting components; active
arrangements also require actuation and control.        The suspension configurations
reviewed by Bouton and Gao illustrate this tradeoff between terrain adaptation and
mechanical complexity [23].
       Neither articulation nor suspension alone establishes adequate clearance or
stability. Otsu et al. show that clearance estimates for articulated rovers depend
on the body pose produced by terrain contact [24]. Papadopoulos and Rey likewise
relate tipover stability to the support contacts, center of mass, and external forces and
moments [25]. Applied to the crawler, these findings favor low equipment placement
within the supported footprint while preserving space beneath the pickup and frame.
Hose forces must also be considered because their direction and attachment location
can produce a tipping moment.
       A rigid chassis is therefore a reasonable practical choice for supporting the


                                           24
vacuum equipment with a simple mounting arrangement. It avoids the additional
mechanisms and motion allowances associated with articulation or suspension. This
choice accepts a limited ability to accommodate uneven ground: wheel contact,
underside clearance, and stability must still be assessed during obstacle crossing.


2.32    Wheeled versus Tracked Locomotion

Wheels and tracks offer different compromises between traction and mechanical
complexity. Wheels provide separate ground contacts, while tracks distribute contact
along a continuous belt. Mandow et al. identify generally better traction from
tracked locomotion, together with greater mechanical complexity [26].          For the
annulus crawler, wheels provide a practical arrangement around the equipment-
carrying frame. A tracked alternative would require space for the track path and
its supporting and driving components.         The traction advantage must therefore
be weighed against integration requirements; neither arrangement establishes pipe-
crossing capability without considering its geometry and contact conditions.
       The selected four-wheel arrangement uses left-right differential drive with fixed
wheel axes. Differences between the speeds on the two sides turn the crawler through
skid steering, which requires lateral wheel slip. Opposite drive directions permit
turning within a small footprint without separate steering hardware, although actual
motion depends on ground contact [26]. This is relevant to maneuvering within the
annulus, where turning space is restricted. Skid steering also introduces resistance
during turns: Shamah’s same-rover comparison on natural terrain found higher power
and wheel torque than with explicit wheel steering, particularly in tight turns [27].
       Compliant tires provide local deformation at the contact without introducing
chassis suspension.   Their selection must account for both forward traction and
lateral sliding during turns. Mandow et al.’s tests with solid-rubber and pneumatic
tires showed that tire construction and inflation affected skid-steering behavior;


                                          25
the tested wheel sets also differed in diameter, so the results do not establish a
universally preferable tire material [26]. For this crawler, compliance is a means of
accommodating local contact geometry, rather than a guarantee of improved traction
under every condition.
       Wheel diameter, tire deformation, and available drive torque also influence
obstacle traversal. Wilhelm et al. relate step climbing to obstacle height relative to
wheel radius and show that a larger radius does not necessarily reduce the required
axle torque. In tests with a driven, foam-filled tire, deformation stored and released
elastic energy, affecting climbing under an abrupt torque input [28]. These findings
support considering wheel size and compliance together. Larger wheels also consume
deployment space, while tire deformation changes the loaded wheel geometry; both
must be considered when selecting clearance for the crawler.
       The rigid four-wheel platform retains limitations that compliant tires cannot
fully overcome. Tire deformation provides only local accommodation and cannot
independently reposition a wheel as a suspension can. Uneven loading or loss of
contact can therefore reduce the traction available for climbing. The pickup and
underside must also clear the obstacle as it passes between the axles; front-wheel
ascent alone does not establish successful traversal. In addition, the sharp step
modeled by Wilhelm et al. differs from a protective pipe: the pipe presents a curved
contact surface and requires descent after the crest [28]. Published step-climbing
results consequently inform wheel selection but do not establish this crawler’s pipe-
crossing limit.


2.33     Summary and Design Implications

The reviewed findings support a rigid chassis with four-wheel differential/skid steering
and compliant tires as a practical configuration for carrying the vacuum equipment
and maneuvering through the annulus. The arrangement combines fixed equipment


                                          26
mounting with steering through the drive wheels and local compliance at the tires.
Its main limitations remain uneven wheel loading, turning resistance, and clearance
and stability during pipe crossing.   Chapter 3 presents the selected dimensions,
construction, and implementation; Chapter 4 describes evaluation with the complete
crawler and its attached hose.

debris. The purchased unit’s exact model and geometry must be recorded before
applying any model-specific catalog data.

4.41      Hand Calculations and Connection Checks

Use the defined force and moment to estimate section tension, bending, and shear
before numerical simulation. For a suitable prismatic section under axial load and
one-axis bending, nominal normal stress is σ = Fn /A ± M cs /I, where A is net area, I
is the second moment of area, and cs is distance from the neutral axis. This nominal
estimate does not resolve local hole or fillet stress concentrations; membrane and
bending stress must be distinguished from such peaks [37].

relevant, and the surrounding material’s bearing, net-section, tear-out, and thread-
pullout limits. NASA’s fastener manual provides the load-group and combined-
loading framework [38]. Preliminary stresses include Nb /At in bolt tension, Vb /Av in
shear, and Vb /(td) in plate bearing, using the appropriate tensile/shear areas, plate
thickness t, and bolt diameter d. Do not assume all bolts carry equal load under an
eccentric pull. If welded, assess the actual weld group and effective throat instead;
neither connection type is yet confirmed.
